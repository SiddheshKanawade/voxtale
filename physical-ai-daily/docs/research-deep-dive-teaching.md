# How to explain one research story to engineers: evidence review

Compiled 2026-11-24 for the new Pixels2Actions long format: one story per video, 12–20 minutes (first case: MIT's "RoboBuilder", where AI coding agents design robots that are then built and tested). The viewer is an engineer working in robotics or AI. The brief from the user: keep the full depth and make the explanation easy to follow, without assuming the viewer is dumb.

**How this was researched.** Web searches, then the primary pages and PDFs themselves: meta-analyses, the original papers, and the creators' own pages and interviews. Pages that blocked plain fetching (PMC, ScienceDirect, Springer, Wiley, SAGE, ResearchGate) were read through Firecrawl. Where only a summary or an abstract could be read, the table says so. Nothing below is quoted from memory. See also `docs/attention.md` (decoration, background audio, cue design), which this doc does not repeat.

**Strength labels** (same as the other research docs)
- **strong**: meta-analysis or several consistent RCTs with an outcome close to ours (learning from narrated visuals).
- **moderate**: RCTs that are small, or in a different setting (children, text instead of video, short lab lessons).
- **weak**: a single small study, a correlational study, or a secondary report I could not check against the original.
- **practitioner**: a creator's or expert's own statement of method, with no test behind it.

**The big caveat.** Almost all of this research uses short lab lessons (minutes, not 15 minutes), mostly students who are novices, and tests learning, not watch time. None of it was run on YouTube or with working engineers. Mayer himself says most of the supporting work comes from short-term lab studies. Our own retention curves and comments will outrank every row below once the format is live.

## 2. Rules for the script

### Structure of a 12–20 minute single-story video

1. **Use a fixed spine.** Hook → the problem and why it is hard (how it's done today, what limits it) → the key idea in one sentence → how it works (2–3 segments) → the evidence → the limits → what it changes → close on the key idea. This is the Heilmeier questions (P5) in a research-talk order (P4). Kilcher's "find the buried core idea" (P7) is the test for the key-idea sentence. *Practitioner, but every source agrees.*
2. **Pick the one thing a viewer should remember. Say it out loud** early, and again at the end. Spend the depth there and treat the rest lightly (P4). Depth comes from going deep on the core mechanism, not from covering every section of the paper. *Practitioner.*
3. **Cut the video into 4–6 minute segments.** Open each with the question it answers, and mark each boundary with a one-line "where we are" signpost (a pointer phrase, a section chip) plus a short pause. Median engagement on MOOC video tops out around 6 minutes (V1). Breaks the designer puts in help, and helped experts at least as much as novices (L7). Verbal signals (outline, headings, pointer words) are among the best-supported cues (L3, d = 0.69). *Moderate.*
4. **The hook is the decisive result plus a specific question.** Show the robot doing the thing, or the surprising number, then ask the question the video answers. Don't open on background, prior work or definitions (P1, P4, P8). Curiosity is highest when viewers are about half-sure (L15), so pick a question an engineer can half-answer ("which of these two designs walked further?"), not trivia and not something obvious. *Practitioner plus moderate lab evidence. No retention data on hooks for long-form exists that we found.*
5. **Re-hook at every segment boundary with a prediction.** Before each key result, ask the viewer to commit to a guess ("what do you think happened on the real hardware?"), hold a beat, then show it. Predictions create the surprise that drives learning (L16). In long real lectures, prequestions mainly help the questioned content (L18), so spend them only on the 3–4 results that matter most. *Moderate in the lab. Untested as rhetorical questions in a video nobody pauses (see section 5).*

### Introducing a concept

6. **Concrete before abstract.** Start with one specific instance: a real design from the paper, a real failure, real numbers. Then state the general method, and name it last (P1: definitions are an end point). If there are two cases, compare them and give the principle *after* the comparison (L13, d = 1.18 for that format). *Strong for comparison; practitioner for the rest. Concreteness fading itself (L21) is mostly theory.*
7. **Show the pain before the solution.** Show the obvious approach and why it fails (for example, a first design that fails in testing, if the paper reports one) before the paper's fix (P3). Problem-first instruction beats instruction-first in classrooms (L12, g = 0.36), but there the learners actually tried to solve the problem; a narrated "you might try X" is a weaker version. *Moderate; extrapolated to video.*
8. **State the expected (wrong) intuition, then break it with evidence.** In a video, refuting a misconception beat a clear exposition (L9, d ≈ 0.8), and experts were not disadvantaged. Refutation works across many studies (L11, g = 0.41, 0.28 after bias correction). Clear expositions feel easy and teach little (L10). **Constraint:** the intuition must be real and sourced: the paper's own framing, its related work, a reviewer, a named researcher. Never invent a straw man (channel rule 1.3: nothing generated). *Strong.*
9. **First-order model first, then add terms one at a time.** Give the simplest version of the mechanism that is still true, then add each complication with its reason (P3). Each step should depend only on the one before (Karpathy's ramp). *Practitioner.*

### Numbers

10. **Every headline number goes on screen, with its comparison.** Pair each result with what it beats or misses: the baseline, the prior best, a human, chance, the number of trials (pattern only, placeholders: "N of M real builds walked; the baseline managed K of M"). Spoken information is transient, and long spoken stretches overload working memory (L8). Printed words help with technical content (L4). Restating a number as a ratio or a familiar unit improves recall and estimation (L20). This matches channel rule 2.7: numbers as words in the narration, digits on screen. *Moderate.*
11. **One number per spoken sentence, and give the denominator** (n, sim vs real, trials). An expert's first question is "out of how many?" (P4: a bright audience will find out; P7: don't trust unverified results). *Practitioner plus moderate (L8).*

### Jargon for engineers

12. **Sort every term into one of two bins before writing.** (a) *Standard in the field* (policy, reward, sim-to-real, PID, transformer, URDF): use it without defining it. (b) *Paper-specific, or from a neighbouring field* (the paper's own module names, a term from LLM-agent work that a controls engineer may not know): define it once, at first use, in one clause, with the term on screen, then use exactly that term every time. The reasoning: support that is redundant for experts hurts them, especially when they can't skip it (L2). Pre-training helps novices but may do nothing for experts (L4). Expertise is task-specific (L2), so even a senior engineer is a novice in the paper's new pieces, and a brief definition is cheap insurance because helping novices matters more than sparing experts (L1). The one-clause format is our extrapolation. *Strong for the principle.*
13. **Never open a section with a definition.** Let the term arrive at the end of the example ("that loop is what the paper calls…") (P1). Say the objective with no jargon first (P5), then bring the precise terms in. *Practitioner.*
14. **Sound like a colleague talking at lunch, not a teacher.** Second person and plain verbs ("you'd expect the agent to…"), no "don't worry, this is easy", no hype adjectives. Conversational style has a large lab effect (L3, d = 1.00), but Mayer notes it may not hold for experienced learners or long lessons (L4), so keep the warmth and lose the cheerleading. Karpathy's version: explain it the way you would over lunch (P3). *Moderate.*

### Analogies

15. **For engineers, the best analogy is often another technical system they know.** A pattern, not a claim about any paper: "this is [a method you know], except [the one thing that differs]." Comparing a known case with the new one is well supported (L13). Use an everyday analogy only when no technical one exists. *Strong for comparison.*
16. **Map the analogy out loud, then say where it breaks.** Name the parts ("the simulator plays the role of…"), say which *relation* carries over, and in the same breath name one place it fails. The analogy you choose drives the inferences viewers make, including the wrong ones (L14). Glynn's Teaching-With-Analogies model has an explicit step for saying where the analogy fails; its page returned 404, so it was seen only in search summaries. *Moderate (small N) plus practitioner.*

### Limits and caveats

17. **Give limits their own segment, before the close, and source each one.** Use the paper's own limitations, failure cases in its videos or appendix, the sim-only vs real-hardware split, sample sizes, and independent replications or critiques with names attached. Don't conceal known problems (P4), and answer the risks question (P5). Engineers discount a story that hides its limits (P7). No limits we made up (rule 1.3). *Practitioner.*
18. **Hedge once, precisely, at the claim** ("in simulation only; N designs were built", with the real numbers), not with vague qualifiers sprinkled everywhere. No study tested this; it follows from rule 11 and P4. *Practitioner/extrapolation.*

### Recaps and retrieval

19. **Recap as a question, then the answer.** At the close, ask two or three "why" questions the viewer can answer from the video ("why did the second design survive the real test?"), pause, then answer each in one line. Quizzes between segments cut mind wandering and improved learning (L19), and generative activities work (L3, d = 0.71), *but in both cases learners actually answered*. A rhetorical question is a much weaker version. Keep mid-video recaps to the one-line signposts of rule 3; re-explaining is redundant for experts (L2). *Strong in the lab; weak for our format.*

### Voice and pacing

20. **Keep 1.15×, and add pauses instead of slowing the voice.** Pause at segment boundaries, after a dense sentence, and after each prediction prompt. Up to 2× playback costs little comprehension (V2), and faster speakers hold MOOC viewers better (V1). Pauses the designer adds act as system-paced segmentation (L7). V2 warns the result may differ for complex material, so watch retention on the dense mechanism segments. *Moderate.*
21. **A modern neural TTS voice is probably fine for learning.** The classic voice-principle studies used old engines (L3, d = 0.74). Modern engines matched or beat human narrators in two studies, one of them an Azure neural voice (V3, V4), but a small 2026 study disagrees (V5). Watch for flat delivery over long technical stretches and fix it with punctuation and pauses. *Mixed; small samples.*

## 3. Rules for the visuals

1. **Build every diagram step by step, in sync with the words.** Each part appears when the narration names it and not before. Narration in sync with the animation is the strongest single effect in the set (L3, d = 1.31), and Mayer's own example of segmenting is a complex slide broken into progressively presented parts (L5). Watching a diagram being drawn helped low-knowledge learners (L5, d = 0.58), and tablet-style drawing held MOOC viewers better than slides (V1). Animated "draw-on" reveals are our closest equivalent; that step is an extrapolation. A build-up also satisfies the channel's 5–7 s visual-change rule (3.2) without cutting away. *Strong.*
2. **Put labels on the part, not in a legend or the caption band** (L3, spatial contiguity d = 0.82). *Strong.*
3. **One signal at a time, and only on what the narration is discussing right now** (a colour fill, a dimming of everything else). Signals help most for complex figures and for the novices in the audience, and work best used sparingly (L4, L6). `docs/attention.md` already found that stacked cues and arrows can distract. *Strong.*
4. **Match the visual to the job of the sentence.**
   - *Mechanism:* the diagram, with the active part highlighted.
   - *Evidence:* the real thing (footage of the build, the plot from the paper), credited on screen.
   - *Number:* the number and its comparison (script rule 10).
   - *Limit:* the actual failure (the fall, the sim-to-real gap plot).

   Never put generic B-roll under an explanation. Interesting but irrelevant material lowers learning, most of all in a system-paced video (L3, L4: coherence d = 0.86). *Strong.*
5. **Don't narrate what the picture already shows an engineer.** Let the diagram carry the "what" and spend the narration on the "why". For experienced learners, a narrated walk-through of a diagram became redundant, and the diagram alone did better (L2). Slides should emphasise the speech, not repeat it (P4). *Strong for direction.*
6. **Rebuild paper figures; don't paste them whole.** Crop to the panel under discussion, or redraw it and reveal one series at a time. About six or seven things on screen at once is the limit (P4), and extra material hurts (L3). Show the original figure briefly with a credit chip, so viewers can see that it's real. *Moderate plus practitioner.*
7. **Equations: only the one that carries the key idea.** Build it term by term, naming each term as it appears, and say it in words too. A term spoken while its symbol appears is temporal contiguity (L3). Showing the whole equation at once while talking about it is the split-attention case. *Moderate, extrapolated.*
8. **Every movement on screen has a purpose** (P1). No drift, no decorative motion (see `docs/attention.md`). *Practitioner plus strong lab evidence on decoration.*

### On-screen text vs narration

9. **Keep the captions.** They are a channel rule (5.1) and an accessibility baseline (V8), they help non-native viewers most (V6), and Mayer's updated count puts the redundancy cost at only d = 0.10 across 12 tests, with reversals for second-language viewers (L3, L4). Mayer's 2021 video paper still recommends against captions that repeat the narration, from four experiments (L5). So keep them compact: one or two lines in a fixed bottom band, clear of the diagram area. *Moderate; see open questions.*
10. **No third text stream.** Besides captions, on-screen text is labels, the key term at its first definition (L4: printed words help with technical terms), and numbers. Never a sentence that repeats the narration. Redundancy hurts most when the screen text is long and verbatim, and least when it is short or reworded (L4). *Moderate.*

## 4. What to avoid with expert viewers (expertise reversal)

The research says techniques that help novices can hurt experts (L1, L2). The cost to experts is smaller than the benefit to novices (L1), and every expert is a novice in the paper's new pieces (L2). So the aim is not "no support". It is support that is brief, targeted at what's new, and easy to pass over.

| Avoid | Why | Do instead |
|---|---|---|
| A long narrated walk-through of a diagram an engineer can read | For experienced learners the narrated explanation became redundant and the diagram alone did better (L2) | Show the diagram and narrate only the non-obvious relation |
| Defining field-standard terms (policy, reward, sim-to-real) | Pre-training may not apply to high-prior-knowledge learners (L4); redundant support costs experts (L1) | Define only paper-specific terms, once, in one clause (script rule 12) |
| Step-by-step derivations of standard methods | The worked-example advantage disappears, and can reverse, with expertise (L2) | Show the result of the standard step; spend the time on the novel step |
| Everyday analogies for things engineers already know | The analogy steers inferences (L14) and adds redundant material (L2) | Compare with a known technical system (script rule 15) |
| Several recaps that re-explain the same point | Redundancy relative to what the viewer already holds costs working memory (L2) | One-line signposts; one question-style recap at the end |
| Highlighting everything; arrows on arrows | Signals mostly help novices and work when sparse (L4, L6) | One signal, on what is being discussed now |
| Cheerleading ("don't worry, this is easy"), hype adjectives | The personalization benefit may not hold for experienced learners (L4); it reads as talking down | Plain, collegial second person (script rule 14) |
| Hiding or softening limits | Experts will find them out (P4, P7) | A sourced limits segment (script rule 17) |
| Decorative B-roll and seductive details | Coherence effects are strongest in system-paced video (L4) | Footage only as evidence (visual rule 4) |
| Showing every step of a procedure an engineer could picture | Imagining a procedure beat studying it for knowledgeable learners, and hurt novices (Cooper et al. 2001, in L2) | Ask the viewer to picture or predict the next step, then show only the surprising part |

## 5. Open questions and weak evidence

- **Expert, long-format, YouTube: no direct evidence.** The expertise-reversal studies use trainees in a few lab sessions. The multimedia principles come from lessons of a few minutes. Mayer notes most of the work is short-term lab research (L3). Nothing tested a 15-minute narrated explainer on working engineers. Treat every rule as a strong prior to check against our retention curves.
- **Captions vs redundancy for native-speaker experts: unresolved.** Mayer's own estimate has shrunk: four experiments at d = 0.80 in the 2021 video paper (L5), 12 tests at d = 0.10 in the 2023 chapter (L3). The caption literature is broad but leans on non-native and special populations (V6, V7). We also add a word-synced caption highlight (channel rule 6.2), which may pull the eye from the diagram to the caption band. Possible test: the same episode with captions burned in vs a YouTube CC track only, comparing retention on the dense segments.
- **Rhetorical prediction and recap questions in a video nobody pauses.** Every benefit in L16–L19 came from learners who actually answered. Whether "what do you think happened?" plus a one-second beat does anything is untested. A pinned comment ("post your guess before 6:30") would be closer to the real thing.
- **Problem-first (L12) in video form.** The meta-analysis is about learners who actually tried to solve the problem first. Narrating the failed obvious approach is our adaptation, not a tested one.
- **Concreteness fading (L21).** The paper read here is theoretical. I did not verify a pooled effect size. The advice to go concrete before abstract rests mainly on practitioners (P1, P4) and on case comparison (L13).
- **Advance organizers.** The classic meta-analyses (Luiten et al. 1980; Stone 1983) could not be accessed, so no effect size is claimed. The support for a short roadmap comes from verbal signaling (L3, initial outlines).
- **TTS voice (V3–V5).** Small samples, mixed results, and no study of edge-tts AndrewNeural or of the 1.15× rate. The Azure "Guy" result (V4) is the closest match.
- **Segmenting helped experts more (L7).** This is the opposite of the usual expertise-reversal pattern, and the abstract doesn't explain why.
- **Personalization for experts (L4).** The boundary is stated, not quantified. Where "conversational" turns into "talking down" for engineers is a judgment call.
- **Practitioner advice (P1–P9) is survivorship.** These creators succeeded, but their rules were never tested against alternatives. Kilcher's view comes from a second-hand write-up. Branch Education's method could not be found. Brunton's and Welch's statements are thin.
- **Not read directly:** Carpenter & Toftness 2017 (only the Learning Scientists summary); Muller's thesis numbers (only Norris & Soloway's summary); Glynn's analogy model (CSUN page 404); the Craig & Schroeder 2017 full text (abstract only). Several meta-analyses were read only as abstracts (L6, L7, L12).
