# Storytelling craft for the ear and the screen: techniques from audio, documentary and YouTube guides

Compiled 2026-11-25/26 for the Pixels2Actions deep-dive format: one research paper per video, 12–20+ min, engineer
viewers, faceless TTS narration, Remotion visuals. It answers the user's diagnosis of the current scripts, "it's not
narration, it's story telling", and the request to "go through story telling guides and then improve".

**Builds on, does not repeat:** `docs/research-deep-dive-storytelling.md` (IDs N#, M#, F#, C#: the evidence that
narrative helps, but/therefore, ABT, the story spine, try-fail, the story circle, Cialdini's mystery, and the Muller,
Harris, Asianometry, Fong and Welch Labs quotes). This doc covers the craft instead: what storytellers in audio, documentary
and YouTube actually do, and how. Where a technique deepens an earlier ID, it says so.

**The failure these guides name is the one we have.** Bernard describes documentaries that are "little more than
illustrated research papers created to 'show' or 'prove' something" ([Bernard 2011, ch. 1](https://booksite.elsevier.com/samplechapters/9780240812410/02~Chapter_1.pdf)).
Glass cut a Sinatra history as "just like the damned Discovery Channel" ([Current 1998](http://www.current.org/people/p809i1.html)).
Glass says NOVA's narrators "accidentally make it feel like school" ([Transom 2011](https://transom.org/2011/ira-glass-radiolab-appreciation/)).
Rober warns against "curiosity-murdering phrases like, 'consider a rigid container of volume V.'" ([TED 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RDFGkBE2O50)).
Every technique below is somebody's answer to that problem.

**Method.**
- **How it was read:** four strands were read in parallel on 2026-11-25/26: audio and radio; documentary and
  documentary ethics; YouTube science and engineering explainers; YouTube documentary creators and retention analysts.
- **Sources read:** about 100 primary pages, transcripts and caption files, saved locally while the work was done.
  YouTube talks were read from captions only, with no video or audio downloaded.
- **Quotes:** each strand string-checked its own quotes against the saved text. I then re-checked a sample of 54 across
  the three subagent strands, plus every quote from my own documentary sources, and all matched.
  - Quotes are verbatim and under 15 words. Anything longer is marked as a paraphrase.
  - Caption timestamps are given as [mm:ss] or [h:mm:ss].
  - Auto-captions can mis-hear words. One known case is Harris's "red", captioned as "read".
- **Access:** the ladder step that reached each source is in the register at the end (`docs/blocked-sources.md`).
  - No paywalled or members-only content was used.
  - Book content comes only from the publisher's free sample chapter, author-adapted excerpts, the author's talks and
    interviews, or reviews. Each use is marked.

**Strength labels.**
- **Primary:** the guide-maker's own words about their own craft. That includes their article, talk or interview, or an
  excerpt of their book published by them or with their permission.
- **Secondary:** a reliable third party reporting what someone said or did. Examples are a review, a profile, Glass
  reporting a colleague's rule, or a blog transcription of a book.
- **Opinion:** an analyst's or commentator's view of what works for other people, not their own documented practice.
  Examples are Galloway or Film Booth on other creators' videos, or Glass on Radiolab. Where an analyst cites retention
  data, the entry says whether the data was shown.

**The big caveat** (as in the earlier doc, section 6). This is practitioner craft. None of it has been tested on a 12–20
min explainer for engineers.
- The retention analysts say they have studied thousands of curves but showed none.
- Several techniques come from traditions with looser truth rules than ours. They are flagged ⚠ and discussed under
  "Where they conflict".

**Reading each entry.**
- **What:** the technique in one or two lines.
- **How:** how the source says to do it.
- **Example:** the source's own example.
- **For a paper video:** our reading. This is framing, not a sourced fact.
- **Sources:** the sources, each with its strength.

## 1. The spine: one question, one train

### V1. One driving question, simple enough to say in a sentence
- **What:** a single "why" or "how" question pulls the listener through and decides what is in and out. Complexity is
  allowed only on top of a simple line. Deepens C1 and C5.
- **How:**
  - Smith: build in "a question that drives listeners through the story", and "promise and deliver an answer that is
    surprising in some way".
  - MacAdam: ask "why?" or "how?", because "These words open doors to a narrative". Her prompts include "What will you
    leave out?"
  - Joyce: if you can't sum the story up in one sentence, "you're headed for trouble".
  - Bernard, under "Think easier": the best recent films are "built on a narrative train that is very basic". The fifth
    edition adds that "a simple narrative throughline can convey complexity".
  - Gibney (paraphrase): you can be as complicated as you like as long as you don't break the simple story.
- **Examples:**
  - Planet Money's dollar-coin episode states its question outright.
  - LEMMiNO's JFK video: "the question I was trying to answer was, 'What happened?'" [00:07:29]. It was not who did it.
    His first draft had reached about 50,000 words (paraphrase).
  - Gibney's *Taxi to the Dark Side* rests on "who killed Dilawar?"
- **For a paper video:** the question is one a curious engineer would ask about the robot, not "what does the paper
  propose". It is said in the viewer's words (see V42).
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Smith, [NPR Training](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/29/g-s1-66327/understanding-story-structure-in-4-drawings) (2016-03-02, republished 2025).
    - MacAdam, [NPR Training](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/30/g-s1-65796/want-razor-sharp-focus-in-your-audio-stories-this-group-activity-can-help) (2018-09-24).
    - Joyce, [NPR Training](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/31/g-s1-65875/campfire-tales-the-essentials-of-writing-for-radio) (2016-03-24).
    - Bernard, [*Documentary Storytelling* 3rd ed., ch. 1](https://booksite.elsevier.com/samplechapters/9780240812410/02~Chapter_1.pdf) (2011). Book excerpt: the publisher's free sample.
    - [Routledge listing, 5th ed.](https://www.routledge.com/Documentary-Storytelling-Creative-Nonfiction-on-Screen/CurranBernard/p/book/9781032267296) (2022). The publisher's description.
    - Gibney, [DGA Quarterly](https://www.dga.org/craft/dgaq/issues/0904-winter-2010/independent-voice-alex-gibney) (Winter 2010). Interview.
    - LEMMiNO, [5M Q&A](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nx4V9Uh_FW0) (2023-10-01).
  - Secondary: Blumberg's "a very basic, very dumb question at the center of a story", via a [CreativeLive blog](https://www.creativelive.com/blog/alex-blumberg-storytelling-tips) (2019-02-20).

### V2. The train and the detours
- **What:** get a forward-moving story going. Once it is moving, you can leave it for exposition and theory, as long as
  you come back. This is the documentary answer to "where does the explanation go?"
- **How:**
  - Bernard, in her 2016 analysis checklist: look for "a forward moving story, or train, that makes you want to keep
    watching". Then ask "where and in what context does it return?", and "Is it there at the film's end?"
  - Her second edition, as transcribed on a 2009 blog, credits the term to Ronald Blumer and Muffie Meyer of Middlemarch
    Films. Once the train is going, "you can make detours as needed for exposition, complex theory". The trick is to
    "get back 'on track' in a reasonable period of time"; otherwise the detours "will seem unfocused".
- **Example (same transcription):** a chronological Jim Jones biography is dull. Start instead with the congressman
  flying to Guyana. The audience will then follow you back through decades of backstory.
- **For a paper video:** the train is the team's attempt to answer V1's question. Background, prior work and the maths
  are detours, and each one ends by naming the train again.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary: Bernard, ["Keep a Close Watch"](https://www.documentary.org/column/keep-close-watch-analyzing-documentarys-strengths-and-weaknesses), IDA, 2016-05-17. An excerpt adapted by the author from the 4th edition.
  - Secondary: [majowords blog](http://majowords.blogspot.com/2009/01/engglish-3.html) (2009-01), a transcription of the 2nd edition. Not checked against the book.

### V3. The plumb line: one protagonist's path through the big subject, and cut what's off it
- **What:** you can't cover a whole subject. Follow one protagonist's path through it, and cut good material that
  doesn't lie on that path.
- **How:** Audette (FRONTLINE editor):
  - The protagonist is "a single path, no matter how complicated and twisted". It runs through "that sphere of the
    larger story".
  - Because of that, viewers "will forgive the film for not covering the entire subject".
  - He hangs a plumb line between himself and the director. A story that is "extremely interesting" but "off the plumb
    line of our protagonist" gets cut.
- **Examples:**
  - Audette:
    - *The Man Who Knew* follows John O'Neill through the FBI's failure before 9/11.
    - An election-night Clinton sequence was cut from *Trump's Road to the White House*.
  - Von Einsiedel (via Bernard 2016): in *Virunga*, poaching and deforestation are kept as context but "minimized" in
    favour of the oil-and-war story.
  - McManus (Real Engineering): a draft covering the top five innovations in aviation got convoluted (paraphrase). He
    "just cut it down to one" [00:19:48].
  - Muller: since "everything is always important all of the time", "rank your top three" [00:29:08].
  - Dettmer cuts any fact "not actually contributing to the understanding" [00:18:09].
- **For a paper video:** prior work, extra benchmarks and side results stay only if they move V1's question.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Audette, interviewed on [Frame.io](https://blog.frame.io/2017/06/21/3-principals-story-driven-pbs-documentary/) (2017-06-21).
    - Bernard ([IDA 2016](https://www.documentary.org/column/keep-close-watch-analyzing-documentarys-strengths-and-weaknesses)).
    - McManus, [Digital Voices](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_RDzBDTWJEo) (2019-05-09).
    - Muller, [Sigma Xi](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SRXeWdfIGrM) (2014-06-24).
    - Dettmer, [Max Planck PhDnet](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_7qRTNJ864U) (2022-02-26).

### V4. Sequences as mini-stories, with hard edges between them
- **What:** each chapter is its own small story, with a beginning, middle and end. The order of the chapters is
  itself an argument.
- **How:**
  - Bernard: find sequences "akin to chapters" with "a unique focus and a clear beginning, middle and end". Then check
    whether "the ordering of these sequences also advances the overall narrative".
  - Smith: every act needs "A change of scene. A change of character. A change of focus." He names shapes such as
    Sarasohn's "Problem » Solution » Complication » Future", and the reporter as detective ("Why is this weird thing
    happening?").
  - Audette: get the order right on paper first, using about 15 "story boxes" (index cards of beats). Only then go back
    and make the trims.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Bernard ([IDA 2016](https://www.documentary.org/column/keep-close-watch-analyzing-documentarys-strengths-and-weaknesses)).
    - Smith, ["How do you tell a story in 3 acts?"](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/30/g-s1-66190/you-asked-how-do-you-tell-a-story-in-3-acts) (2017-11-10).
    - Audette ([Frame.io 2017](https://blog.frame.io/2017/06/21/3-principals-story-driven-pbs-documentary/)).

### V5. Test the structure by telling it aloud
- **What:** the order you naturally use when you tell the story to a person is usually its best structure.
- **How:**
  - Glass: stuck producers are asked to put their notes down and "just tell us the story". This lets him hear the
    structure they naturally use.
  - TAL's submissions page asks for a pitch told "the way you'd tell it to someone over drinks or coffee".
  - The Moth: "Imagine you are at a dinner party, not a deposition."
  - Glass also writes a version with all his favourite moments, then reads it to people (Abel's book).
- **For a paper video:** before outlining, the writer tells the story from memory in two minutes. That recording is the
  first outline.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Glass, [Transom manifesto](https://transom.org/2004/ira-glass/) (2004).
    - [TAL Submissions](https://www.thisamericanlife.org/about/submissions) (undated).
    - [The Moth tips](https://themoth.org/share-your-story/storytelling-tips-tricks) (undated).
    - Abel, *Out on the Wire*, excerpt on [Lit Hub](https://lithub.com/keep-or-kill/) (2015). A book excerpt.

### V6. "Discovery fiction": start from the naive, flawed solution and refine it, and say that's what you're doing
- **What:** walk from an obvious but flawed approach to the real one, so each design choice arrives as the fix to a
  visible flaw. It deepens F4 (try-fail) and the earlier doc's rule that a logical order may differ from the paper's.
- **How:** Grant Sanderson:
  - "start with a naive but flawed solution, and then progressively refine it" (SoME2 [00:09:50]).
  - He calls it "This genre of discovery fiction" (SoME3 [00:17:58]). It works best where a topic has many seemingly
    arbitrary choices (paraphrase).
  - The path "doesn't have to match the actual history" (JMM [00:57:41]). The condition is being "very honest" with the
    audience about where those falsehoods are [00:58:32].
- **For a paper video:** the naive approach is the obvious one a practitioner would try, or the paper's own baseline.
  When the order is ours, the narration says "suppose you…", not "first they tried".
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Sanderson, [SoME2 results](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cDofhN-RJqg) (2022-10-01).
    - Sanderson, [SoME3 results](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6a1fLEToyvU) (2023-10-07).
    - Sanderson, ["Math's pedagogical curse"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UOuxo6SA8Uc) (JMM, uploaded 2023-04-01).

## 2. Character: who the story is about

### V7. A faceless voice needs people in the story even more
- **What:** facts alone don't hold an audience. A narrator without a face has to bring in the people behind the work.
- **How:**
  - Tom Scott: speaking as "an anonymous voice or a corporation" [00:44:38], you have to "tell people about the human
    story that's in it" [00:44:49].
  - Glass: "Every story had to have someone who was more than a talking head", and "it sometimes only took a line of
    description". He counts "no sympathetic character" among the reasons a story isn't worth doing.
  - Kern (via MacAdam): "make them care about someone in your story".
  - MacAdam: "Humanize the experts!"
- **For a paper video:**
  - Name the researchers. Give each a line of sourced description: their role, what they did, what they said in a talk
    or thread.
  - Their inner states stay unsaid unless they are on record.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Tom Scott, ["There is No Algorithm for Truth"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=leX541Dr2rU) (Royal Institution, 2019-10-24).
    - Glass ([Transom 2004](https://transom.org/2004/ira-glass/)).
    - MacAdam, ["From print to radio"](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/30/g-s1-65814/the-journey-from-print-to-radio-storytelling-a-guide-for-navigating-a-new-landscape) (2017-12-06).
  - Secondary: Kern, via ["How a long audio story is different"](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/31/g-s1-66564/how-a-long-audio-story-is-different-from-a-short-one) (2015-03-09).

### V8. Scientists as characters: the method is the plot
- **What:** a scientist's sequence of moves is the story, told as actions. This matches N3 and N6 (narratives about real
  scientists did better).
- **How:**
  - Krulwich told Caltech graduates about Mary Schweitzer, who worked "with bones and dead birds in buckets, patiently
    looking for patterns". He admits it is harder "to add faces, and motives, and football analogies to electrons".
  - NOVA's Julia Cort: "There has to be motivation, a question, and then steps along the way". The steps include "ups and
    downs, challenges, dramatic tension, and then a resolution".
- **Example (Krulwich):**
  - A T. rex bone fragment arrives in the mail, and Schweitzer says it came from a pregnant female.
  - She calls ostrich breeders and collects a dead ostrich from a farmer's backhoe bucket.
  - The result is a paper in *Science*.
- **For a paper video:** the protagonist is the team doing documented things: it measured, built, filmed, threw away.
  The paper's logs and appendix are the plot.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Krulwich, ["Tell Me a Story"](https://calteches.library.caltech.edu/711/2/Story.pdf) (Caltech commencement 2008-06-13; printed Fall 2008).
    - Cort, in [GBH's NOVA 50th piece](https://wgbh.org/foundation/highlights/2024-03-05/50-years-of-science-discovery-celebrating-novas-signature-storytelling) (2024-03-05).

### V9. Someone has to change: find the pivot
- **What:** a story needs a change in a person, and an identifiable point where it turns.
- **How:**
  - Catherine Burns, as drawn by Abel: "You need the person to undergo a change." Abel's page marks "The pivot point of
    this story."
  - Bernard's working questions: "Who are the central characters? What do they want?" and "What are the stakes if they
    don't get it?"
- **Example (Abel's excerpt, the Moth story):** an annoyed flight attendant learns that the passenger guarding the
  overhead bin is carrying his dead son's uniform.
- **For a paper video:** in a paper, the change is usually in belief. What did the team, or the field as the paper
  frames it, think at the start? What did the evidence force them to think by the end? Both ends must be sourced.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Abel ([Lit Hub excerpt, 2015](https://lithub.com/keep-or-kill/)). A book excerpt.
    - Bernard ([ch. 1 sample, 2011](https://booksite.elsevier.com/samplechapters/9780240812410/02~Chapter_1.pdf)).

### V10. The narrator is a secondary character; the subject is primary
- **What:** the host guides, and the subject and its people are the protagonists. A template protagonist with a problem
  is not the only way in.
- **How:**
  - Cleo Abram: "consider myself like a secondary character that's taking you" [00:40:26], with "an infinite well of
    interesting primary characters" [00:42:37].
  - Galloway on Ryan Trahan's growth videos: "the subject is not a personality" [00:02:09].
  - Kurzgesagt rejects the "this is mr. Miller mr. Miller has a problem we solve" template [00:23:27]. Their
    maternal-mortality video still opens on a situation: "something strange was going on in the world's largest
    Maternity Hospital in Vienna" [00:11:21]. It then brings in Semmelweis, the person who investigated.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Abram, [Colin and Samir](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a9sdUTsnFnE) (2024-09-17).
    - Patrizia Mosca (Kurzgesagt executive producer), [Internetdagarna](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Qy4OARwQuC8) (2018-11-20).
  - Opinion: Galloway, ["How Ryan Trahan Got 1000% More Views"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxoCyoV3LBY) (2022-02-08).

### V11. ⚠ Personifying a non-human protagonist, and the version we can use
- **What:** Rober names and personifies his subject, and lets the viewer discover things alongside him.
- **How:**
  - "when you can buy into the character and i could personify them" [00:09:13].
  - "i should bring the viewers on this same journey with me of discovery" [00:09:52].
- **Example:** the squirrel "Fat Gus" turned out to be a pregnant female, and that discovery became a beat.
- **For a paper video:** this conflicts with N3 (humanized actors made science narratives worse) and with the channel
  rule against giving a robot or agent wants.
  - The usable half is the shared discovery.
  - The robot gets literal action verbs from the logs, such as "the agent asked for bench tests". It gets no feelings.
- **Sources:** primary: Rober, ["Reveals Secret Science of Viral Videos"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Pe7tgNflaeg) (Jon Youshaei, 2021-11-17).

## 3. Scenes vs summary: making a moment concrete

### V12. A scene is something happening, specifically. Summary is the bridge between scenes
- **What:** a scene is time, place, actor and action, shown. A scene is not just a setting.
- **How:**
  - MacAdam:
    - "Scenes are not the same thing as narrative." Something has to happen.
    - "The more show, the less tell required, the better", and "You should be moving."
    - "Too many scenes are mediocre because they are predictable."
  - Updike praises Scott Carrier's one-line details as "tiny, powerful scenes".
  - The Burns team writes narration as scene. For Julia Ward Howe, awake at night: "she got up and scribbled out the
    words with a pencil stub", as quoted in NEH.
- **Examples:**
  - Frank Morris walks the seven-mile path of the Joplin tornado.
  - MacAdam regrets one of her own stories, which spent 6 of its 12 minutes explaining a premise before any scene
    (paraphrase).
- **For a paper video:** a scene is a documented moment with a time, a place and a doer. For example, one robot, one
  course and one try, with what the camera shows. Pure summary should be the bridge between scenes, not the body.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - MacAdam, ["How audio stories begin"](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/28/g-s1-67588/how-audio-stories-begin) (2016-07-26).
    - MacAdam, ["6 NPR stories… neighborhood scenes"](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/31/g-s1-67543/6-npr-stories-that-breathe-life-into-neighborhood-scenes) (2015-10-29).
    - Updike, ["Better Writing Through Radio"](https://transom.org/2006/nancy-updike/) (Transom, 2006-01-24).
  - Secondary: Tonguette, [*Humanities* (NEH)](https://www.neh.gov/humanities/2015/septemberoctober/feature/the-civil-war-ken-burns-reinvented-the-television-history-d) (Sep/Oct 2015).

### V13. Anecdote, then a moment of reflection
- **What:** a run of events is followed by a line saying what it means. Repeat. Sometimes add a "zoom-out" from the case
  to how the world works.
- **How:** Glass:
  - "Because you can't just have an anecdote. It's got to mean something."
  - His staff ask "what's the anecdote and then where's the moment of reflection".
  - A story fails "if the story doesn't lead to some interesting idea about how the world works".
  - Blumberg, as reported by Glass: "this little thing is an example of this bigger thing".
- **Examples:**
  - A stranger on a subway platform tells each person "You can stay" or "you gotta go". Glass reflects: "There is just
    something about the judgment of strangers."
  - Sedaris's Macy's elf piece runs "anecdote, reflection, anecdote, reflection".
- **For a paper video:** after each scene, one sentence of meaning. That sentence must be the paper's finding or a
  framing clearly marked as ours (see V54).
- **Sources:**
  - Primary: Glass, ["Mo' better radio"](http://www.current.org/people/p809i1.html) (MPR lecture 1998-02-11, *Current* transcript 1998-05-25), and [Transom 2004](https://transom.org/2004/ira-glass/).
  - Secondary: Blumberg, via Glass.

### V14. Experience before explanation ("hide the vegetables")
- **What:** get attention with something the viewer can see or feel, then attach the learning to it. Don't open with
  the formalism.
- **How:**
  - Abumrad sees "a grand-canyon sized gulf between explanation and experience". Rather than explain a dolphin's sonar,
    he has listeners imagine being one: "So imagine, you're this dolphin".
  - Rober: "I can't teach you if I don't have your attention." [00:04:27]. He calls it "hiding the vegetables"
    [00:05:22].
  - Film Booth: "You gotta give them a burger and put some broccoli in it."
- **For a paper video:**
  - Show the robot doing the thing, or failing to, before any definition.
  - The definition arrives as the answer to "what just happened?"
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Abumrad, [Transom manifesto](https://transom.org/2012/jad-abumrad-gut-wrench/) (2012-07-26).
    - Rober, [TED](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RDFGkBE2O50) (2026-05-27).
  - Opinion: Ed Lawrence (Film Booth), [Creator Science ep. 155](https://podcast.creatorscience.com/ed-lawrence/) (2023-07-04).

### V15. One concrete case carries the abstraction
- **What:** one specific, vivid case beats an aggregate as the way in. The aggregate comes after.
- **How:**
  - Gibney opened *Enron* on one executive's suicide. One death is "far more poignant or haunting than the collapse of a
    company"; it "allowed us time to get into some of this other stuff".
  - Sanderson: "keep one or two examples front and center", and "motivate using clear examples, not sweeping statements
    or promises of what is to come".
  - Muller warns against "burying the lead" [00:26:08]; his rule is "lead with awesome" [00:26:15].
- **For a paper video:** open on one robot's documented failure, then give "N of M" right after. Pairing the vivid case
  with its denominator is the earlier doc's rule (N1, N10).
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Gibney ([DGA 2010](https://www.dga.org/craft/dgaq/issues/0904-winter-2010/independent-voice-alex-gibney)).
    - Sanderson ([SoME2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cDofhN-RJqg)).
    - Muller ([Sigma Xi 2014](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SRXeWdfIGrM)).

### V16. Show the wrong turns and false starts
- **What:** the messy attempts are the scenes. A clean answer has none.
- **How:**
  - Sanderson praises "a pretty authentic look at the wrong turns and tangents" (SoME2), and "certain false starts in the
    discovery process, which I love" (SoME1 results). He also values "the various ideas that he tried, the reasons that
    they didn't work" (SoME3).
  - Rober: "let me show you how hard it is" [00:14:30], and "a lot of times i'll show my failures" [00:14:40].
- **Example:** Steve Mould tells the chain-fountain story through his failures.
  - "I have absolutely no idea why it happens" [00:06:23].
  - His own equations fail, then Reddit, then "a slow motion video doesn't help" [00:08:46].
  - The answer comes from two Cambridge academics.
- **For a paper video:** papers hide wrong turns, so use the ones on record. These are ablations, rejected variants,
  early rounds, appendix failures, and author talks. Never invent one.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Sanderson ([SoME2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cDofhN-RJqg); [SoME1 results](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=F3Qixy-r_rQ), 2021-10-23; [SoME3](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6a1fLEToyvU)).
    - Rober ([Youshaei 2021](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Pe7tgNflaeg)).
    - Mould, [TEDxNewcastle](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wmFi1xhz9OQ) (2014-07-03).

### V17. "Look at this": write to the picture, and let it do what words can't
- **What:** narration points the eye at the evidence, then gets out of the way. Words explain why, pictures show what.
- **How:**
  - Harris: "I'm not going to say something unless I have a visual that can dance" [00:30:19].
  - Caswell on Joss Fong's "Look at this": "That is a magic trick that not enough people use".
  - Ward (Burns's writer): "A lot of writing film is removing things." Also: "You find that the images can replace
    narration." Pictures can't explain a complicated diplomatic story, though, so writing has to say why.
  - Joyce: "Let the sound come in first; then refer to it."
  - A critic on *The Civil War*'s photograph of a scarred back, which the narration never mentions: "reticence can be its
    own emphasis" (George Will, quoted by NEH).
- **For a paper video:** the TTS line names what to watch ("watch the left wheel") and stops. Cut any sentence that only
  describes what the clip already shows.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Harris, [Jon Youshaei](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fa1_VWuIElE) (2024-11-12).
    - Caswell, [School of Motion](https://www.schoolofmotion.com/blog/estelle-caswell-vox-podcast) (2019-06-26).
    - Ward, [amNY](https://www.amny.com/opinion/a-conversation-with-the-writer-of-ken-burns-s-documentary-the-vietnam-war-1-14288127) (2017-09-28). Interview.
    - Joyce ([NPR 2016](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/31/g-s1-65875/campfire-tales-the-essentials-of-writing-for-radio)).
  - Secondary: Will, via [NEH 2015](https://www.neh.gov/humanities/2015/septemberoctober/feature/the-civil-war-ken-burns-reinvented-the-television-history-d).

## 4. The dramatic question, open loops and re-hooks

### V18. Two layers of motivation: a hook for the whole, a reason for every new idea
- **What:**
  - A macro hook pulls the viewer into the lesson.
  - A micro motivation gives each new idea a reason to arrive. It deepens C1 ("introduce the science right when you need
    it").
- **How:** Sanderson:
  - The macro hook is about "how well do you hook someone into the lesson as a whole" [00:03:10].
  - The micro motivation builds the intuition first, so the formal idea lands on "something that already exists at least
    loosely in the viewer's mind" [00:08:46].
  - "Motivation is critical, but it doesn't have to take long" [00:07:53].
  - "The best hook in the world is wasted" [00:11:09] if what follows is unclear.
- **Sources:** primary: Sanderson ([SoME2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cDofhN-RJqg)).

### V19. "What happens next": chronology and small loops
- **What:** order pulls by itself, and short raise-and-answer loops hold attention inside a section.
- **How:**
  - Glass: "You just cannot help but want to know what happens next." Also "If nothing else occurs to you, just tell it
    in order", and "unanswered questions equal suspense" (Abel's book).
  - Joyce: "Raise a question in the listener's mind." Then: "And answer it with tape if possible."
  - Film Booth: "you open a loop, so you pose a question, then you explain it" [00:25:10]. Also "If you open a loop, you
    probably want to close it." [00:48:14]. On dragging out a payoff: "we don't want to do is annoy people and drag it
    out" [00:56:13].
- **Example:** Joyce plays a walrus sound before naming it.
- **For a paper video:** tell events in order only when a source states the order (earlier doc, section 4, rule 1).
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Glass ([Current 1998](http://www.current.org/people/p809i1.html); [Lit Hub excerpt](https://lithub.com/keep-or-kill/)).
    - Joyce ([NPR 2016](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/31/g-s1-65875/campfire-tales-the-essentials-of-writing-for-radio)).
  - Opinion: Film Booth, ["How To Write Scripts Better Than 99%…"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a5438jzwVnQ) (2023-10-26). The backlash anecdote is his only evidence.

### V20. Promise the top two or three questions, then pay each one off by name
- **What:** state the questions up front and say they will be answered. Each one then gets its own payoff.
- **How:**
  - Film Booth: make sure "your packaging poses three big questions to your viewers" [00:19:02]. List and rank them,
    promise the top three in the setup, and have each section pay one off.
  - Muller opens with "Am I retiring? That is the question I will answer in this video" [00:00:00]. He then adds "you
    can skip ahead to the answer if you like".
  - Sanderson: "I won't tell you the final decision until the end of the video" [00:02:34].
- **For a paper video:** this is our three cold-open questions. The test is whether each is answered in a chapter that
  names it.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Muller, ["The Future of Veritasium"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=piHGnG4LsmQ) (2025-12-24).
    - Sanderson ([SoME2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cDofhN-RJqg)).
  - Opinion: Film Booth ([2023](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a5438jzwVnQ)).

### V21. Re-hook at the start of every section, and hand over the next storyline at once
- **What:** section boundaries are where viewers leave. Open each section with a new question, and don't pay off its
  point in the first line.
- **How:**
  - Film Booth: "we want to re-hook at the start of every point" [00:27:18], and "you do not pay off the point
    instantly" [00:27:38].
  - Galloway: "at this point we want to give the viewer the next story line" [00:19:51]. His list of "retention danger
    zones (transitions, end of storylines, heavy context scenes)" says to keep them short.
  - Kern (via MacAdam): in long pieces, "The characters and subject are frequently reintroduced", because "Not everyone
    starts listening with the intro."
- **Sources:**
  - Opinion:
    - Film Booth ([2023](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a5438jzwVnQ)).
    - Galloway, [The Editing Podcast](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Q0zRBsYQnA0) (2022-10-11). He claims "three four five thousand retention curves" [00:06:27], none shown.
    - Galloway, ["20 notes" thread](https://threadreaderapp.com/thread/1811083485840302211.html) (2024-07-10).
  - Secondary: Kern ([NPR 2015](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/31/g-s1-66564/how-a-long-audio-story-is-different-from-a-short-one)).

### V22. A mystery the viewer investigates: the detective structure
- **What:** frame the video so the viewer is trying to solve something. It deepens C1 and F6.
- **How:**
  - Gibney: "Creating a sense of mystery allows the viewer to start investigating". At some point you stop laying out
    "This happened and then this happened" and decide how to tell it.
  - Smith lists the reporter-as-detective shape: "Why is this weird thing happening?"
  - Adam Davidson frames a Planet Money story as a genre: "Our story is The Bourne Identity, it's a caper flick."
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Gibney ([DGA 2010](https://www.dga.org/craft/dgaq/issues/0904-winter-2010/independent-voice-alex-gibney)).
    - Smith ([NPR 2017](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/30/g-s1-66190/you-asked-how-do-you-tell-a-story-in-3-acts)).
    - Davidson, in Abel's *Out on the Wire*, excerpt in [Nieman Reports](https://niemanreports.org/how-radio-reporters-turn-ideas-into-feelings/) (2015-08-25). A book excerpt.

## 5. Setups, payoffs and callbacks

### V23. Set up the evidence so it lands, then don't step on it
- **What:** tell the viewer what they need in order to get the most from the clip, number or quote. Then let it hit,
  and don't restate it.
- **How:**
  - Updike: "Don't repeat the tape." "Let the tape have the money shot." The script should give listeners "what they
    need to know to get the most out of the tape".
  - Joyce: don't "step" on your acts.
  - NPR Training: "Begin a thought and let the tape complete it".
- **Examples (Updike):**
  - Alix Spiegel first says that an EMS crew chief has worked 20 years without trouble. So his "I cry every day" lands.
  - Alex Chadwick sets up "calm, quiet, serious scientists" who "go wild". The subdued Mars-rover tape then reads as wild.
- **For a paper video:** our "tape" is the clip, the plot and the paper's own sentence.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Updike ([Transom 2006](https://transom.org/2006/nancy-updike/)).
    - Joyce ([NPR 2016](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/31/g-s1-65875/campfire-tales-the-essentials-of-writing-for-radio)).
    - MacAdam, ["Writing through sound"](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/29/g-s1-66787/writing-through-sound-a-toolbox-for-getting-into-and-out-of-your-tape) (2015-06-23).

### V24. Plant early, remind, pay off late, and escalate toward the hardest test
- **What:** plant a detail early and remind the viewer it's coming. Make each step raise the stakes of the next.
- **How:**
  - Galloway: "Foreshadow what's to come", and "remind viewer of the 'pay off' or reason to keep watching". His example of
    a plot hole: "introducing a storyline that was never closed later in the video".
  - Galloway's Red Bull case: "Early action - Drag race (this serves as a benchmark)", then Build, Test and Final lap, so
    that "everything works together to make the end more high stake and climatic" [sic].
- **Example:** Destin Sandlin plants his grandfather's "I love you to the comet belt" [00:14:31] with "More about that
  later" [00:14:43]. It returns during a delta-v calculation for Mars: "Oh my goodness, the comet belt" [00:17:29].
- **For a paper video:** show the baseline's failure early as the benchmark, and keep the hardest evaluation for last.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary: Sandlin, [TEDxVienna](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wcWD7fERQCc) (2014-12-11).
  - Opinion:
    - Galloway (["20 notes"](https://threadreaderapp.com/thread/1811083485840302211.html); [Red Bull thread](https://threadreaderapp.com/thread/1782802698150048114.html), 2024-04-23). Based on a review of his own client's video; no curve shown.

## 6. Stakes: why the viewer should care

### V25. Something has to be at stake: an "unresolved something"
- **What:** a narrative needs a conflict or an open problem at its centre. Good material without one isn't a story.
- **How:**
  - Julie Snyder, as reported by Glass: "in a narrative story, something has to be at stake".
  - Glass, rejecting a truck-driving documentary: "There's no unresolved something at the center of it."
  - The Moth: "A story without stakes is an essay".
  - Sarasohn: "Tell me what the stakes are."
- **Example:** reporters have pitched TAL stories about sandhogs (tunnel diggers), praising the great voices. Glass's
  question back was "what's the conflict, what's the drama?"
- **For a paper video:** the stake is a documented cost or consequence (hours of real testing, failures on hardware),
  at the scale the source claims.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Glass ([Current 1998](http://www.current.org/people/p809i1.html); [Transom 2004](https://transom.org/2004/ira-glass/)).
    - [The Moth](https://themoth.org/share-your-story/storytelling-tips-tricks).
    - Sarasohn, in ["Vocabulary for an audio editor"](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/30/g-s1-66719/vocabulary-for-an-audio-editor-15-things-to-say-over-and-over) (2015-05-14).
  - Secondary: Snyder, via Glass.

### V26. Never seen before, relevant to the viewer, and made tangible by analogy or case
- **What:** pair novelty with relevance, and make abstract stakes concrete through an analogy or a case study.
- **How:**
  - Muller's two prongs: "showing something people have never seen before" [00:21:40], and "something that's relevant
    to people's lives" [00:21:47].
  - Tom Scott on aquarium life support: "if it breaks, they'll die. That's a spaceship" [00:07:53].
  - Grady Hillhouse's topic filter asks for "a cool case study that would kind of show people the stakes" [00:26:58]. He
    also had to "recalibrate the things that I find exciting" [00:21:59].
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Muller ([Sigma Xi 2014](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SRXeWdfIGrM)).
    - Tom Scott, [WVFRM](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LRi-CrzY6d4) (2022-06-03).
    - Hillhouse, [YouTube Creators Hub](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HDixkBct8Yw) (2026-10-02).

### V27. Turn the idea into a feeling: frame the abstract
- **What:** science lands when it's tied either to the listener's own life or to a big question about the world.
- **How:**
  - Soren Wheeler (Radiolab): science can be brought back to something that "either goes direct and personal or goes big
    and philosophical", and "you have to turn it into a feeling".
  - Adam Davidson: "You have to frame the story."
- **Example:** a Radiolab epigenetics story about rats licking their pups. Abumrad: "it's so easy to get lost in the
  mechanics". The parenting frame was placed midway through, and it became "a story about hugging your kid".
- **For a paper video:**
  - The engineer's "direct and personal" frame is their own bench, for example anyone who has trusted a simulator.
  - The frame must not raise the stakes beyond the source (earlier doc, section 4, rule 8).
- **Sources:** primary: Abel, *Out on the Wire*, excerpt in [Nieman Reports](https://niemanreports.org/how-radio-reporters-turn-ideas-into-feelings/) (2015-08-25). A book excerpt.

### V28. A testable "why" with a genuine outcome
- **What:** give the video a claim to test, and let the outcome really be open. Viewers lose interest the moment they
  suspect it's rigged.
- **How:**
  - Galloway on Trahan: "he instantly gives the video a why" [00:04:48].
  - Sam Denby on Jet Lag: "as soon as the audience thinks that we're doing that, it becomes uninteresting" [00:17:11].
- **For a paper video:** show the real test, failures included, and don't foreshadow a win the data didn't give.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary: Denby, [Oxford Union](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_q8k3qV8Aqg) (2026-05-12).
  - Opinion: Galloway ([Trahan, 2022](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxoCyoV3LBY)).

## 7. Reveals and twists without manipulation

### V29. Misconception first, then the correction
- **What:** voice the plausible wrong idea fairly, then correct it. It deepens the earlier teaching rule 8.
- **How:** Muller's PhD work:
  - "a clear expository summary is worse than no instruction at all" [00:04:10].
  - In the version that worked, "I got one to voice the common misconceptions" [00:04:24].
  - Students called it confusing [00:04:56], yet learned more (paraphrase).
  - His rule: "you always have to start with the misconceptions" [00:06:03].
- **Examples:**
  - Sanderson praises a plane-pitch lesson that begins "a lot of people assume that this is the Doppler effect".
  - Galloway's best hook set up a common belief and then flipped it: "oh wait but there's more to this story"
    [00:05:45].
- **For a paper video:** the misconception must be documented, for example in the paper's framing, its related work or
  a named researcher. It must not be a straw man (earlier doc, section 4, rule 3).
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Muller, [TEDxSydney](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RQaW2bFieo8) (2012-06-25).
    - Sanderson ([SoME2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cDofhN-RJqg)).
    - Galloway, on his own channel ([Jon Youshaei](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AX3dEGjBg6w), 2023-04-02).

### V30. Give away the what, then sell the how and why ("Jenga")
- **What:** pay off the promise right away, then go back. The suspense moves from what happened to how it happened.
  This matches N14's curiosity order.
- **How:**
  - Rober: "pay off on that thumbnail in the first 30 seconds" [00:05:53]. Then "before we get to that we first need to
    go back eight months" [00:06:03]. As he puts it, "you know how the game of jenga is going to end" [00:06:27].
  - Updike: "very few radio stories are whodunits"; what holds people is how and why it happened (paraphrase).
  - Glass sometimes uses "the very last scene as the opening" (Abel's book).
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Rober ([Youshaei 2021](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Pe7tgNflaeg)).
    - Updike ([Transom 2006](https://transom.org/2006/nancy-updike/)).
    - Glass ([Lit Hub excerpt](https://lithub.com/keep-or-kill/)).

### V31. Make the difficulty clear before the reveal, and earn the emotional beat
- **What:** an unimpressive-looking result needs its difficulty set up first. Emotion, or music that signals it, only
  works once the context has earned it.
- **How:**
  - Fong on the first black-hole image: she wanted viewers to "understand why this is so difficult before they saw the
    result". Then she built "a reflective moment around that".
  - Harris: "feely music is dangerous" [00:33:41], and "if you haven't earned it, it then is tacky" [00:33:53].
- **For a paper video:** robot results often look slow or clumsy. Set up why the task is hard before the clip plays.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Fong, [The Open Notebook](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/) (2020-01-07).
    - Harris ([Youshaei 2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fa1_VWuIElE)).

### V32. Build in surprise, and keep delayed reveals honest
- **What:** surprise is engineered by breaking a pattern. Withholding a fact is legitimate only when it doesn't leave a
  false impression.
- **How:**
  - Glass: "We are really careful to build surprises into This American Life." He also tries "to express my own
    amazement when I am amazed".
  - Smith: "When a pattern is briefly interrupted, we react with surprise."
  - Glass admits a delayed label: he held back that a girl named Sylvia was Mexican, because "we consciously manipulate
    the facts to allow you entrance".
  - MacAdam warns that this kind of thing is "misdirection", and says to "Always consider the expectations you're
    planting in the listeners' mind."
- **For a paper video:**
  - A delay is fine if every sentence is true at the moment it's said.
  - "Surprising" needs a source (earlier doc, section 4, rule 4).
  - See "Where they conflict", item 3.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Glass ([Current 1998](http://www.current.org/people/p809i1.html)).
    - Smith ([NPR 2016](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/29/g-s1-66327/understanding-story-structure-in-4-drawings)).
    - MacAdam, ["'Once upon a time'…"](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/28/g-s1-65252/once-upon-a-time-and-other-devices-for-starting-your-story) (2015-02-18).

### V33. Complication is the twist: let the contradicting fact touch the good scene
- **What:** the most interesting turns are facts that complicate the tidy version. Burns builds his films on them.
- **How:** Burns:
  - "the real genuine stories are about one and one equaling three"; "the good guys have really serious flaws"; "my
    interest is always in complicating things".
  - His edit room has a neon sign reading "it's complicated". As he puts it, "the process of learning for us is to learn
    contradictory and complicating information".
  - Facts come first, "even if those facts are often contradictory and destabilizing of an already good scene".
- **Example (*On Story*):** Jefferson wrote "all men are created equal" while owning slaves, and never freed one in his
  lifetime. Burns: "that's a good story".
- **For a paper video:** a limitation that changes what a result means (for example, a validation set that also chose
  the fixes) is a turn. Place it where it bites (see "Where they conflict", item 9).
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Burns, [*Ken Burns: On Story*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlZYgPllKNU) (Redglass Pictures; YouTube upload 2015-12-18).
    - Burns, [Conversations with Tyler](https://conversationswithtyler.com/episodes/ken-burns/) (2022-11-16).
    - Burns, quoted in [Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2026/ken-burns-american-history-documentary-filmmaking/) (2026-06-23).

## 8. The narrator's stance and voice

### V34. Choose a stance: the authority on the hill, or the learner reporting a discovery
- **What:** a narrator can explain from above, or report a path they walked. The second carries built-in humility.
- **How:** Sanderson:
  - The distanced narrator is "standing on top of a hill and explaining the way that things are" [00:14:54]. That needs
    you to "know 10 times as much" [00:15:01] as you say.
  - The alternative is "discovery journalism" [00:15:13], which has "this inbuilt piece of humility" [00:15:32].
  - His test: "does it feel like an actual person is talking to you" (JMM [00:19:04]).
- **For a paper video:** our "we" is a reader who went through the paper and is reporting what it found, not a lecturer.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Sanderson, [SoME1 announcement](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ojjzXyQCzso) (2021-07-16).
    - Sanderson, [JMM 2023](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UOuxo6SA8Uc).

### V35. The narrator says why they care, and "I" earns its keep
- **What:** a narrator who says what they wanted to know gives the listener a reason to follow. First person is used
  only when it serves the viewer.
- **How:**
  - Koenig's colleagues on early *Serial* drafts: "I'm bored unless you tell me what you think", and "I need to know why
    you care". Technical, uncertain material needs "a strong hand telling you what to listen for and why".
  - Sean Cole: "'I' has to earn its keep", and "The reporting is the action of the story."
  - Harris: first person only "in service to the viewer and in service of the story" [00:34:13].
  - MacAdam: go personal when "The story stems from your own curiosity", and use it sparingly.
- **For a paper video:**
  - "We" may say what we wanted to find out and what to watch for.
  - "We" never claims feelings about the result, and never claims anyone else's feelings.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Koenig, [Austin Chronicle](https://www.austinchronicle.com/arts/unpacking-serial-11764540) (2015-07-30).
    - Cole, ["All in Favor… Say I!"](https://transom.org/2009/sean-cole-say-i/) (Transom, 2009-02-13).
    - Harris, [Jerusalem Press Club](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lRq6rIiGFAU) (2019-07-08).
    - MacAdam ([NPR 2016](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/28/g-s1-67588/how-audio-stories-begin)).

### V36. Getting lost, then found: admit the confusion
- **What:** being confused, out loud, is a structure, not a weakness. It deepens C4 (Fong's hosts as stand-ins).
- **How:**
  - Abumrad: every Radiolab story is "an act of getting lost and then being found". Also: "it felt more honest to be more
    confused in our stories".
  - Abram: "start with the question that I came in with" [00:22:53], and "I try to actually show grappling with a
    difficult concept" [00:44:25].
  - Izzy Harris: "the ability to say like I don't know" [00:42:59].
  - Glass on Radiolab: two narrators can "pause on a difficult idea and bring up opposing arguments".
- **For a paper video:** a single TTS narrator can voice the obvious objection or question ("but wait: if the
  simulator said yes, why…?") and then follow it.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Abumrad, [Indy Week](https://indyweek.com/news/culture/theater/interview-jad-abumrad-reveals-inner-workings-radiolab-and-creative-dark-side/) (2014-10-29).
    - Abumrad, [Longform #383](https://longform.org/posts/longform-podcast-383-jad-abumrad) (Mar 2020).
    - Abram ([Colin and Samir 2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a9sdUTsnFnE)).
    - Johnny and Izzy Harris, [Colin and Samir](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mFRMvhY9ajE) (2025-03-04).
  - Opinion: Glass ([Transom 2011](https://transom.org/2011/ira-glass-radiolab-appreciation/)).

### V37. Keep a log of your own confusion before the curse of knowledge sets in
- **What:** write down what confused you on first contact, then script for that earlier self.
- **How:**
  - Grey: "being confused and frustrated about a topic is a fundamental part of" writing about it [00:48:24]. In final
    drafts he checks "what past me was confused about" [00:48:58].
  - Harris: "write down what it felt like to not understand it" [00:18:41].
  - Fong, on ML papers: "I feel lost and stupid with a first read" [00:12:46]. Later she can "retrace my steps of
    understanding" [00:28:16], because "the second you learn something you become less effective at explaining it"
    [00:28:01].
  - Hank Green: "The most interesting thing was like four steps back".
  - Dettmer asks readers "do you get what we are saying in this script" [00:28:21].
- **For a paper video:** the researcher's first-read questions, in order, become the script's open questions.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Grey, [Cortex #9](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HjOz7dupcME) (2015-12-14).
    - Harris ([Youshaei 2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fa1_VWuIElE)).

### V38. A third-person narrator with a chorus of first-person voices
- **What:** an even narrator carries the line, and the participants' own words supply the texture.
- **How:**
  - Burns was advised early that "third-person narration, complemented by a chorus of first-person voices" could hold an
    audience.
  - NEH describes McCullough's narration as "authoritative without ever being attention-hogging".
- **For a paper video:**
  - The TTS narrator is the even voice.
  - The authors' own sentences (paper, talks, threads), shown as attributed on-screen quotes, are the first-person
    chorus.
- **Sources:**

## 9. Writing for the ear

### V39. One idea per sentence, said the way you'd say it
- **What:** the ear can't re-read, so each sentence carries one new idea in spoken English.
- **How:**
  - Kern (via MacAdam): "time goes in only one direction".
  - Sarasohn: "Each sentence should only have one new idea in it." Also: "That's something you would write that you
    would not say."
  - Joyce: "Use short sentences." "Verbs are better than adjectives."
  - Tom Scott: "I write in roughly the same way I talk" [00:25:10].
  - Hillhouse unlearned engineering speech, where "we talk in jargon we use very formal language" [00:20:10].
  - Fong: "you can't really afford to be too clever or beautiful when you're doing voice-over".
- **Example:** Steve Inskeep's spoken lede, "It involves cricket.", set beside an AP print lede.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Sarasohn ([NPR 2015](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/30/g-s1-66719/vocabulary-for-an-audio-editor-15-things-to-say-over-and-over)).
    - Joyce ([NPR 2016](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/31/g-s1-65875/campfire-tales-the-essentials-of-writing-for-radio)).
    - Tom Scott ([WVFRM 2022](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LRi-CrzY6d4)).
    - Hillhouse ([2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HDixkBct8Yw)).
    - Fong ([Open Notebook 2020](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)).
  - Secondary: Kern, via ["Would you say it that way?"](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/31/g-s1-66074/would-you-say-it-that-way-tips-on-writing-for-your-voice) (2015-02-25).

### V40. Read it aloud as a separate draft, and kill what's lifeless
- **What:** hearing the script is a different test from reading it, and some scripts fail it.
- **How:**
  - Grey: reading aloud "my brain seems to count as a totally different thing" [00:57:01]. It focuses him on "what is the
    rhythm of this sentence" [00:57:21]. His late-stage kill test is a script where "it just has no life to it"
    [01:08:48].
  - Grey's essay: the goal is "Clearer. Shorter.", ending with "800 to 1,200 coherent and interesting words" after 30–50
    drafts.
  - Galloway wrote one intro 12 times and recorded every version [00:04:12]. As a faceless channel, "my words are what
    carries the story" [00:04:22].
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Grey ([Cortex #9](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HjOz7dupcME)).
    - Grey, ["Iteration: Thoughts on Writing"](https://cgpgrey.substack.com/p/iteration) (2015-03-12).
    - Galloway, on his own channel ([Youshaei 2023](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AX3dEGjBg6w)).

### V41. Signposts: tell people what to pay attention to, not how to feel
- **What:** pointing arrows, recaps and hinge lines keep a listener oriented without telling them what to feel.
- **How:**
  - Abumrad: "people don't want to be told how to feel", but they "do want to be told what to pay attention to". His
    example is "and that's the moment when everything changed". He admits they use these "too much perhaps".
  - Smith: "Signposts can recap what we've learned this far."
  - Sarasohn: "Billboard your transitions."
- **Example:** Alix Spiegel's recap line: "So what has Jason learned from all of this?"
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Abumrad ([Transom 2012](https://transom.org/2012/jad-abumrad-gut-wrench/)).
    - Smith ([NPR 2016](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/29/g-s1-66327/understanding-story-structure-in-4-drawings)).
    - Sarasohn ([NPR 2015](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/30/g-s1-66719/vocabulary-for-an-audio-editor-15-things-to-say-over-and-over)).
    - MacAdam ([NPR 2015](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/29/g-s1-66787/writing-through-sound-a-toolbox-for-getting-into-and-out-of-your-tape)).

### V42. Translate the jargon; ask in the viewer's words
- **What:** use metaphor and plain words, and pose the question the way a viewer would ask it, not the field.
- **How:**
  - Krulwich: "As science gets harder, metaphor becomes more useful, and even necessary." He turned electrons into "two
    platoons" on a line of scrimmage.
  - Davidson: "So it's a lot of translation."
  - Glass on Radiolab: "They don't use the word stochasticity."
  - Muller retitled "Are Negative Ions Good For You?" ("a question nobody asked ever" [00:16:51]) as "Do Salt Lamps
    Work?". His condition: "the title and thumbnail accurately describe what the video is about" [00:09:52].
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Krulwich ([Caltech 2008](https://calteches.library.caltech.edu/711/2/Story.pdf)).

## 10. Pacing through variety

### V43. Alternate the modes: anchor and bridge, story and thing; change something every minute or so
- **What:** move between concrete evidence and connecting context, and between story and explanation. Keep changing
  something.
- **How:**
  - Harris: "this idea of anchor bridge anchor bridge is it's my formula" [01:07:57], and "a story can't be all visual
    anchors it needs stitching" [01:07:23].
  - Harris's intro: "I call it look at this thing that's the intro" [01:17:52]. Then the dense context, then back to
    the evidence.
  - Captain Disillusion's checkerboard: "there's the story, there's the thing" [00:16:17].
  - Glass: listeners are "trained to expect something to change every 45 to 50 seconds". He warns against going "script
    to tape to script to tape".
  - Joyce: "Vary lengths of acts and tracks."
  - Bernard: "Share the humor". Audiences can't sit through a programme of "unrelieved misery".
- **Example:** Harris's Colombia/Venezuela border film alternates evidence (crowds, suitcases, worthless bolívar notes)
  with stretches of history.
- **For a paper video:** the anchors are the clips, failure frames and plots; the bridges are method and context.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Harris ([Jerusalem Press Club 2019](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lRq6rIiGFAU)). He self-reports that 70% of viewers finish [01:20:10], with no graph shown.
    - Captain Disillusion, [Digital Spaghetti](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fCewGo4lvNw) (2026-07-16).
    - Glass ([Current 1998, p. 2](http://www.current.org/people/p809i2.html) for the 45–50 s rule; [Transom 2004](https://transom.org/2004/ira-glass/) for "script to tape").
    - Joyce.
    - Bernard ([ch. 1](https://booksite.elsevier.com/samplechapters/9780240812410/02~Chapter_1.pdf)).

### V44. Slow down at the key beats, and pause before a turn
- **What:** give the important lines room. A pause before a turn tells the listener something is coming.
- **How:**
  - Galloway says he has told almost every client to "slow down" [00:00:11]. His version: "Slow down. Give the viewer
    space to breathe."
  - Fong names the risk of "charging forward and not realizing that the viewer is not with you".
  - Biewen: "The pause signals that the plot is about to thicken."
  - Abumrad: sparse music "really focuses your attention on the words and the information".
  - Sanderson's first-video mistake: having "math come at you at this fire hose rate" [00:16:27].
  - Grey plans "what are the Beats of this lesson" [00:29:27].
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Fong ([Open Notebook 2020](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)).
    - Abumrad, [Wheeler Centre](https://www.wheelercentre.com/news-stories/2016/read-jad-abumrad-on-the-evolution-of-radiolab) (2016-12-05).
    - Sanderson ([SoME1 announcement](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ojjzXyQCzso)).
    - Grey ([Cortex #9](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HjOz7dupcME)).
    - Biewen, Transom excerpt via the [Hearing Voices compilation](https://hearingvoices.com/tow/story-structure) (undated; step 5).
  - Opinion: Galloway ([Editing Podcast 2022](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Q0zRBsYQnA0); ["5 tips" thread](https://threadreaderapp.com/thread/1742221356450611588.html), 2024-01-02).

### V45. A visual code, so the viewer always knows where they are
- **What:** a consistent look for each kind of material lets a complicated story stay simple.
- **How:** Gibney shot every Bagram witness in the same half-lit style, so "every time the viewer saw somebody shot like
  that, you would feel, Bagram". As he says, "It helped to simplify the story."
- **For a paper video:**
  - Simulation, real hardware, the paper's figures and our sketches each keep one look across the whole video.
  - A music change can mark a section end (Fong: "beginnings and endings of songs are cues to viewers").
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Gibney ([DGA 2010](https://www.dga.org/craft/dgaq/issues/0904-winter-2010/independent-voice-alex-gibney)).
    - Fong ([Open Notebook 2020](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)).

## 11. Openings and endings that land

### V46. The opening is the film's DNA, and it doesn't start at "the beginning"
- **What:** the opening sets the promise and the rules, and starts in the middle of something. It deepens C1 and C2.
- **How:**
  - Bernard: the opening "contains the DNA of the entire film to come" and "sets out the promise between filmmaker and
    audience". That covers what the film is about, how and with what it will be told, and why it matters.
  - Bernard on *Virunga*: it did not open on archival facts from 1885: "That would have been dull, because we're not yet
    motivated to care." It opened on a funeral scene that raises questions. "We don't know everything, and that's as it
    should be."
  - Glass: "you pull one sexy anecdote out as the bait to put at the beginning". As he puts it, "The beginning really is
    like a trick."
  - Updike: radio "has to frontload the drama".
  - The Moth: "Start in the action."
  - Sanderson's own past mistake was to "over-philosophize in the video's introduction" [00:07:49].
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Bernard ([IDA 2016](https://www.documentary.org/column/keep-close-watch-analyzing-documentarys-strengths-and-weaknesses)).
    - Glass ([Lit Hub excerpt](https://lithub.com/keep-or-kill/)).
    - Updike ([Transom 2006](https://transom.org/2006/nancy-updike/)).
    - [The Moth](https://themoth.org/share-your-story/storytelling-tips-tricks).
    - Sanderson ([SoME2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cDofhN-RJqg)).
  - Secondary: the "point of attack" idea, Bernard quoted in Cynthia Close's [IDA review](https://www.documentary.org/feature/storys-thing) (2004-03-31). A review of the 1st edition.

### V47. Plan the ending first, and bookend with a callback
- **What:** decide the ending before writing the middle, and close by returning to something from the opening.
- **How:**
  - Film Booth: "we need to make sure the ending is planned before the middle". His "loop" is "I do something at the end
    which happened at the beginning".
  - The Moth: "Your last line should be clear in your head before you start."
  - Glass drafts "ending A and ending B" and reads both to people (Abel's book).
  - Galloway on Trahan: "this same storyline is revisited towards the end of the video" [00:07:44].
- **Example:** Krulwich opens on a robin, "a small, feathery, modern dinosaur". He closes on the same robin: a friend
  now sees the T. rex in it, and "you have just placed a sword in her hand".
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Krulwich ([Caltech 2008](https://calteches.library.caltech.edu/711/2/Story.pdf)).
    - [The Moth](https://themoth.org/share-your-story/storytelling-tips-tricks).
    - Glass ([Lit Hub excerpt](https://lithub.com/keep-or-kill/)).
    - Lawrence, on his own practice ([Creator Science 2023](https://podcast.creatorscience.com/ed-lawrence/)).
  - Opinion: Galloway ([Trahan, 2022](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxoCyoV3LBY)).

### V48. Endings resolve the theme, reveal something, and don't overclaim. The back half gets the same care
- **What:** a good ending pays off the opening's promise and shows something new. It doesn't inflate the result, and
  it gets as much craft as the opening.
- **How:**
  - Tompkins: "Good endings resolve the main theme of the story." "It is usually better to end a story with narration."
    "Resist the temptation to make your ending something it isn't."
  - The Moth: "Steer clear of meandering endings".
  - Bernard: "Does the film satisfactorily conclude the story it promised in the opening?"
  - NOVA's Cort: "an ending that reveals something that you didn't know before".
  - Sanderson: "leave any commentary about broader themes and connections to the end" [00:07:59].
  - Audette won't take on a story without "a beginning, a middle, and an END!"
  - Burns: "every single moment have the same attention given to it". He means the end of a 10th episode as much as the
    introduction.
- **For a paper video:** the limits, use and recap chapters are scenes and answers, not lists.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Al Tompkins, course excerpt on [Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/?p=416850) (2016-06-14). Written about TV.
    - Bernard ([IDA 2016](https://www.documentary.org/column/keep-close-watch-analyzing-documentarys-strengths-and-weaknesses)).
    - Cort ([GBH 2024](https://wgbh.org/foundation/highlights/2024-03-05/50-years-of-science-discovery-celebrating-novas-signature-storytelling)).
    - Sanderson ([SoME2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cDofhN-RJqg)).
    - Audette ([Frame.io 2017](https://blog.frame.io/2017/06/21/3-principals-story-driven-pbs-documentary/)).
    - Burns ([Conversations with Tyler 2022](https://conversationswithtyler.com/episodes/ken-burns/)).

## 12. How fact-bound storytellers keep it true

### V49. Creative arrangement, not creative invention: find the story, don't impose it
- **What:** documentary storytelling is choosing and ordering real material, never making things up. It deepens M1–M3.
- **How:**
  - Bernard: "Our stories depend not on creative invention but on creative arrangement". The filmmaker "finds (as opposed
    to imposes) a story". Barnouw, quoted by her, says the documentarist "is dedicated to not inventing".
  - Bernard on how trust is betrayed: by "selecting only those facts that support your argument". Or by "bending the facts
    in service of a more 'dramatic' story".
  - Bernard: story "definitely does not mean artificial tension that is imposed from without".
- **Sources:** primary: Bernard ([ch. 1 and preface, 3rd ed., 2010–2011](https://booksite.elsevier.com/samplechapters/9780240812410/01~Front_Matter.pdf)). A book excerpt: the publisher's free sample.

### V50. The "where did you get that image?" test
- **What:** one test from documentary ethics: would the viewer feel betrayed if you showed how you made it?
- **How:** filmmakers shared the rule "Honor your viewers". What they take as real "wouldn't be betrayed if you told
  them where and how you got that image".
- **For a paper video:** apply the test to every clip (playback speed, which run, which round), every chart redrawn from
  a table, and every sketch.
- **Sources:** primary: Aufderheide, [IDA](https://www.documentary.org/feature/honest-truths-looking-groundbreaking-ethics-report-five-years-later) (2015-04-15). She summarises her own 2009 study *Honest Truths* (45 filmmaker interviews), which was not read in full.

### V51. Label anything reconstructed or animated, make it obvious, and source the claims inside it
- **What:** reconstructions and animations are allowed only when the viewer can tell they're reconstructions, and only
  when they rest on evidence.
- **How:**
  - BBC: "It should be obvious to the audience where a reconstruction begins and ends". Reconstructions rest on "a
    substantial and verifiable body of evidence". Editing "must never be used to give the audience a materially
    misleading impression".
  - PBS: re-creations must be "as accurate as possible and clearly identified".
  - FRONTLINE: "Factual assertion in CGI sequences requires annotation in the script".
  - NPR (Kern): reporters "do not 'manufacture' scenes for news programs". Imagined devices are fine only when it is
    obvious they are imagined.
- **Example (American Experience):** an early animation of the attack on Isaac Woodard showed a full-frame drawing of
  him that could be mistaken for a photograph. It was redrawn from the side and back. "We just don't want people to
  think that we're misleading them."
- **For a paper video:**
  - Remotion diagrams and animated robots are CGI. Every fact in them goes in the source map.
  - Our own sketches say so on screen (we already do this: "Rigid-body engine (our sketch)").
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - [BBC Editorial Guidelines, section 3 (3.4.25, 3.4.30)](https://www.bbc.co.uk/editorialguidelines/guidelines/accuracy/guidelines) (current online edition, read 2026-11-25).
    - [PBS Standards on *American Experience*](https://www.pbs.org/standards/blogs/standards-articles/american-experience-recreations) (2021-10-21).
    - [FRONTLINE/GBH journalistic guidelines](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/frontline/about-us/journalistic-guidelines/) (§38, §59; modified 2025-09-09).
    - [NPR Ethics Handbook, "Honesty"](https://www.npr.org/about-npr/688367308/honesty) (undated; quotes Kern's *Sound Reporting*).

### V52. Quotes: never composite, never re-pair, signal every cut
- **What:** a quotation must be something the person actually said, in context.
- **How (PBS):**
  - "Editing should never alter the meaning of a person's remarks."
  - Don't join fragments into one quote, or edit an answer so it seems to answer a different question.
  - Mark every jump, with ellipses in text or a cue on screen.
- **For a paper video:** a paper quote spliced from two sections is a composite and needs an ellipsis or two cards.
- **Sources:** primary: [PBS Standards memo, "Handling quotations"](https://www.pbs.org/standards/blogs/standards-memos/handling-quotations) (2021-06-24).

### V53. Music and emphasis may not claim what the script can't support
- **What:** music, effects and vocal emphasis count as claims, and have to be backed like any other.
- **How:**
  - FRONTLINE: guard against using music or effects "to communicate something that is not supported in the script".
  - Sanderson: in technical sections, "it's very easy for the music to do more harm than good" [00:12:36].
  - Harris warns that unearned "feely" music is tacky (see V31).
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - [FRONTLINE guidelines](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/frontline/about-us/journalistic-guidelines/) (§43).
    - Sanderson ([SoME2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cDofhN-RJqg)).
    - Harris ([Youshaei 2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fa1_VWuIElE)).

### V54. Distrust the story that fits too well; reflection lines are where invention creeps in
- **What:** the neatest story is the one most worth re-checking. Big summary lines are where untruths slip in.
- **How:**
  - Grey flags stories that are "too cute" [00:44:18], where "it fits the narrative like a little bit too well"
    [00:44:28]. His example is the story of how Uranus was named after King George.
  - Fong: "stories are like sugar for us" [00:38:15]. She describes "this bias toward coherence and narrative", and says
    "the truth is always chaotic".
  - Kurzgesagt: "we often have to change our view on an issue while working on it" [00:01:09].
  - Glass admits that some of his zoom-out lines were knowingly false. Of one: "That is completely untrue, but it really
    sounds good".
- **For a paper video:** V13's reflection sentence gets the same source check as a number.
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Grey ([Cortex #9](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HjOz7dupcME)).
    - Fong ([Gradient 2024](https://thegradientpub.substack.com/p/joss-fong-videomaking-ai-science-communication)).
    - Kurzgesagt, ["How to Make a Kurzgesagt Video in 1200 Hours"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uFk0mgljtns) (2020-02-16).
    - Glass ([Current 1998, p. 3](http://www.current.org/people/p809i3.html)).

### V55. Simplifying is a chosen untruth: say where it is
- **What:** every simplification leaves something out, and the honest move is to make that visible.
- **How:**
  - Dettmer: "you always have to lie when you simplify" [01:02:34]. The real question is "do people agree with the way we
    lied in a video" [01:02:43].
  - Muller: "what you're going for is an emotional truth", but "it needs to be faithful to the ideas" [00:23:58].
  - Sanderson's condition for discovery fiction (V6): be honest with the audience about where the falsehoods are.
  - Burns: "all story is manipulation", and "an emotional truth is something that you have to build". He also says
    "we'll always let the facts rule".
- **For a paper video:** a simplification gets a spoken or on-screen flag ("simplified: the paper also…").
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Dettmer ([PhDnet 2022](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_7qRTNJ864U)).
    - Muller ([Sigma Xi 2014](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SRXeWdfIGrM)).
    - Burns ([On Story](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlZYgPllKNU); [Poynter 2026](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2026/ken-burns-american-history-documentary-filmmaking/)).

### V56. Tag every claim while writing, check characterizations as well as numbers, and correct in public
- **What:** checking runs alongside the writing, covers how things are described as well as the numbers, and
  continues after publication.
- **How:**
  - Harris colour-codes his script: anything needing a fact check stays red while he writes [00:56:05], and turns green
    once it has a source. His checkers "check our characterizations check our numbers" [00:49:01]. He admits "the notion
    of objectivity is is largely a myth" [00:48:05].
  - Grey goes to experts when a script is "about 80% done" [00:46:08]. Simplification, though, stays "a narrator's
    decision".
  - Veritasium: "we also use multiple experts with every video" [00:14:46].
  - Glass, retracting the Mike Daisey story: audiences "take it as a literal truth". Of the source TAL couldn't verify:
    "we should've killed the story right there and then".
  - Corrections in public:
    - Hossenfelder made a video private and named what she missed (it "isn't peer reviewed").
    - Fong calls her own error "the main flaw of the piece".
    - Hank Green: "It's to help people believe the true things."
- **For a paper video:** our sentence tags (earlier doc, section 4) cover events and numbers. This adds
  *characterizations*: is the word choice ("failed", "solved", "first") fair to the source?
- **Sources:**
  - Primary:
    - Harris ([Jerusalem Press Club 2019](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lRq6rIiGFAU); [Colin and Samir 2025](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mFRMvhY9ajE)).
    - Grey ([Cortex #9](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HjOz7dupcME)).
    - Muller ([Future of Veritasium](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=piHGnG4LsmQ)).
    - [TAL #460 "Retraction" transcript](https://www.thisamericanlife.org/sites/default/files/audio/upload/special/TAL_460_Retraction_Transcript.pdf) (2012-03-16).
    - Hossenfelder, ["why I took down my climate science video"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vDsjeKo3u3o) (2025-03-17).
    - Fong ([Open Notebook 2020](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)).

## Where guides agree

1. **One simple question carries the complexity.** Radio, documentary and YouTube all start from one question you can
   say in a sentence. Complexity hangs off it, and what doesn't serve it is cut (V1, V3, V20, V22, V25).
2. **Concrete first, abstract second.** A scene, a case, or "look at this" comes first, and the explanation arrives as
   its answer. Every tradition says not to start at "the beginning" or with the formalism (V12, V14, V15, V17, V18,
   V46).
3. **Alternate action and meaning.** The same alternation appears under different names:
   - Glass: anecdote and reflection.
   - Bernard and Blumer & Meyer: the train and its detours.
   - Harris: anchor and bridge.
   - Captain Disillusion: story and thing.

   No guide lets explanation run for long without a scene to come back to (V2, V13, V43).
4. **Real people doing things.** The method, told as actions by named people, is the plot (V7, V8, V16). This agrees
   with the evidence already on file (N3, N6).
5. **A curious guide, not a lecturer.** The narrator is a secondary character who asks the viewer's questions, admits
   confusion and points attention. Most of them say: don't tell the viewer how to feel (V10, V34–V37, V41).
6. **Write for the ear, and test by listening.** One idea per sentence, spoken English, read aloud, and the structure
   tested by telling it to someone (V5, V39, V40).
7. **Set up, then pay off.** Tell the viewer what to watch for, let the evidence land, and don't repeat it. Plan the
   ending and return to the opening (V23, V24, V47, V48).
8. **Arrangement, not invention.** The documentary bodies (Bernard, BBC, PBS, FRONTLINE, NPR) and the careful
   explainers (Grey, Fong, Hossenfelder, Harris's colour-coding) agree on two things. Story is the order and the joins.
   Anything imagined or reconstructed is visibly labelled (V49–V52, V56).
9. **Cutting is most of the craft.**
   - Ward: "A lot of writing film is removing things."
   - Muller: rank your top three.
   - Grey: "Clearer. Shorter."
   - Audette: cut what's off the plumb line.

   (V3, V40)

## Where they conflict

1. **Withhold the answer, or give it away.**
   - Withhold: MacAdam's mystery structure, Joyce's bit-by-bit reveal, and Fong's delayed black-hole reveal (V19, V31).
   - Give away: Updike ("very few radio stories are whodunits"), Rober's "Jenga" and Glass's last-scene-first (V30).
   - LEMMiNO, as a reader, disliked knowing the ending.
   - The way out, which Updike and Rober both describe: give away *what* happened, and keep *how and why* uncertain.
     For us, the cold open shows the outcome, and key numbers wait for a prediction prompt.
2. **Close loops quickly, or keep the big payoff alive.**
   - Film Booth: close loops reasonably soon and don't drag them out.
   - Galloway: foreshadow and keep reminding the viewer of the payoff.
   - The memory evidence doesn't favour either (N15: no Zeigarnik effect).
   - Compatible reading: small loops close inside their chapter, while the two or three promised questions stay open
     with reminders (V19–V21, V24).
3. **Feelings: say them, or don't.**
   - Glass expresses his own amazement and calls radio "a very didactic medium".
   - Abumrad says people don't want to be told how to feel.
   - Our earlier rule allows "surprising" only when a source says so.
   - For a TTS narrator we take Abumrad's side: point attention and state the sourced idea, but claim no feelings
     (V32, V35, V41).
4. **"I" and narrator presence.**
   - For: Cole ("try 'I'"), Koenig, Caswell's friend-at-a-bar voice, and Abram's on-screen grappling.
   - Limits: MacAdam says to use it sparingly. Harris allows it only in service of the viewer. Galloway says not in the
     intro. Fong is uneasy "extracting anecdotes from people".
   - A faceless channel can use "we" for curiosity and attention only (V34–V36).
5. **Personification.** Rober personifies. N3 and our rule say not to. We keep the shared discovery and describe the
   robot literally (V11).
6. **How much truth latitude.** This is the deepest split.
   - Loose:
     - Herzog: "ecstatic truth… can be reached only through fabrication and imagination and stylization", while cinema
       vérité gives only "the truth of accountants" ([Minnesota Declaration, Walker Art Center, 1999-04-30](https://walkerart.org/reader/minnesota-declaration-truth-documentary-cinema-1999)).
     - Burns: "all story is manipulation".
     - Muller: aim for "emotional truth".
     - Glass knowingly used untrue zoom-out lines, and delayed a label "to allow you entrance".
     - Film Booth uses an invented anecdote and recommends staged reenactments.
     - Galloway: "clickbait by exaggerating but still delivering is the new meta" [00:08:21].
   - Strict: Bernard, BBC, PBS, FRONTLINE, NPR, Grey, Fong and Hossenfelder.
   - For an expert audience, and given N7 (absorbed viewers catch fewer false notes), we follow the strict side and
     borrow only *stylization* that is clearly labelled (V49–V55).
7. **Music.**
   - Kurzgesagt scores every video and claims "30 to 40 percent higher impact" with no source. Vox changes tracks often
     (per Fong).
   - Against: Sanderson says music harms technical sections, FRONTLINE says music must not claim what the script can't,
     Harris says emotion must be earned, and Abumrad says sparse music focuses attention.
   - For us: keep the technical core sparse, and use music changes as section cues (V45, V53).
8. **Pace.**
   - Fast: Rober ("every like quarter second is sacred"), and Galloway's count of a visual change every 1.1 s in MrBeast.
   - Slow: Galloway tells his own clients to slow down, Fong warns about "charging forward", and Biewen pauses before
     turns.
   - Galloway's own reconciliation: fast entries, room to breathe at the key beats (V44).
9. **Where the caveats go.**
   - Our structure so far (earlier doc's chapter 7; script v3's chapter 6) collects every caveat in one late chapter.
   - Burns ("touch" the working scene), Fong ("the truth is always chaotic") and Grey ("too cute") imply something
     different: a caveat that changes a result's meaning belongs inside the scene it complicates.
   - Compatible reading: put meaning-changing caveats where they bite, and keep the late chapter for what's still open
     (V33).
10. **Script first, or find the story in the edit.**
    - Script first: Tom Scott (every word written in advance) and Grey (words locked before animation).
    - Find it in the edit: Rober ("the story is found in the edit"), Sandlin (per Tom Scott), and Bernard ("a
      'script' evolves organically").
    - For us, the "edit" is the research: the story is found in the sources, then scripted, then the visuals push back
      on the words, as Kurzgesagt's illustrators do (V5, V17).
11. **Who explains: the narrator, or the people on tape.** Updike says the narrator should do the explaining instead of
    using "explaining tape". MacAdam says to humanize the experts rather than drop them. For us, the narrator explains,
    and the authors' words are the chorus (V38).
12. **Labelling the topic up front.**
    - Planet Money states its question, and Sarasohn says to "Billboard your transitions."
    - Glass praises Radiolab for never saying "stochasticity", and calls NOVA school-like.
    - Abumrad thinks they over-use pointing arrows.
    - Compatible reading: signpost the question and the turns, not the field's vocabulary (V41, V42).
13. **Length.**
    - Sanderson: "length isn't necessarily the enemy".
    - Grey ("Clearer. Shorter."), Muller ("rank your top three") and Dettmer (about 1,200 words per 10 min) push for
      cutting.
    - For a 12–20 min format, length should come from depth on the plumb line, never padding (V3).

## A storyteller's checklist for a research-paper video

An editor can answer each question by marking up the script. Every "no" points to the technique to apply.

1. **One question.** Can you write the central question in one sentence, starting with "why" or "how", in words a
   viewer would use and with no term the paper coined?
   - Does every chapter's first or last line connect to it? (V1, V20, V42)
2. **Plumb line.** Mark each passage on or off the line.
   - Is every off-line passage either cut, or a detour under about 30 s that ends by naming the train again? (V2, V3)
3. **Scenes vs summary.** Highlight every scene: a named doer, a documented action, a specific time and place, shown on
   screen.
   - Is there a scene in the first 30 s, and at least one per chapter?
   - Is there any run of more than about 60–90 s with no scene or anchor? (V12, V15, V43, V46)
4. **Meaning after action.** Does each scene end with one line saying what it means?
   - Is that line either a sourced finding or a framing that reads as ours, never an untrue zoom-out? (V13, V54)
5. **Who does things.** Count the sentences whose subject is a person or team doing a documented action, against those
   whose subject is "the paper", "the method" or "results".
   - Are the researchers named, with at least one sourced line of description each? (V7, V8, V10)
6. **The change.** Write down the belief at the start and at the end, both sourced. Where is the pivot?
   - Does any sentence give the robot or agent a want or a feeling? (V9, V11, V33)
7. **Motivation before mechanism.** Does every new component, term or equation arrive only after the problem it solves
   has been shown?
   - Could any chapter open with "Now let's look at…"? (V14, V18, V6)
8. **Loop ledger.** List every question the script raises, explicit or implied, with the line that answers it.
   - Is every small loop closed inside its chapter?
   - Is every cold-open question answered by name?
   - Is any storyline never closed, a "plot hole"? (V19, V20, V21, V24)
9. **Setups.** Before each key clip, number or quote, is the viewer told what to watch for?
   - After it, is it left alone rather than restated?
   - Is the difficulty set up before an unimpressive-looking result? (V17, V23, V31)
10. **Stakes.** Within the first two minutes, what is at stake, from a source, at the source's own scale?
    - Is it tied to something the viewer has done themselves?
    - Is the outcome shown as really open? (V25–V28)
11. **The fair misconception.** Is there a documented "obvious" belief, voiced fairly before it is corrected?
    - Is the complication that changes a result's meaning placed where it bites? (V29, V33)
12. **Narrator stance.** Does the narrator say what we wanted to find out, and point attention ("watch…") at least once
    per chapter?
    - Does it voice the viewer's objection at the hard step?
    - Does any line tell the viewer how to feel, or claim a feeling for anyone? (V34–V37, V41)
13. **Variety and pace.** Label each block of about 45–60 s as scene, explanation, reflection or number.
    - Are there ever three of the same kind in a row?
    - Is there a pause before each turn?
    - Read aloud or rendered: does any sentence carry more than one new idea? (V39, V40, V43, V44)
14. **Ending.** Was the last line written before the middle?
    - Does the ending return to the cold-open image or question and resolve it, without overclaiming?
    - Do the back-half chapters have scenes rather than lists? (V46, V47, V48)
15. **Truth audit.**
    - Is every reconstruction, diagram and animation labelled, with each fact inside it in the source map?
    - Are paper quotes free of composites?
    - Does the music claim nothing the script doesn't?
    - Does every clip pass the "where did you get that image?" test?
    - Has the part that fits most neatly been checked a second time? (V49–V56)
