# Storytelling craft from narrative nonfiction and science writing

Compiled 2026-11-26 for the Pixels2Actions deep dives: one research paper per video, 12–20 minutes, engineer viewers, faceless TTS narration. The user's diagnosis after several rewrites: "it's not narration, it's story telling." Our scripts are accurate and logically ordered, but they still play like a lecture or a paper summary. This doc collects the concrete techniques that prose storytellers use for true stories about ideas and discoveries, and says how each applies to a paper story.

**Builds on, does not repeat:** `docs/research-deep-dive-storytelling.md`. That doc covers the evidence (N#), the risks (M#), the fiction and comedy tools (F#: but/therefore, ABT, story spine, try-fail cycles, story circle, Cialdini's mystery), the creator quotes (C#), and the revised episode structure with its factual rules. Entries here say where they extend one of those.

**Strength labels** (as requested):
- **primary:** the author's own words, from their article, notes, blog, talk, interview or book text.
- **secondary:** someone else describing the method.
- **opinion:** an editor's or annotator's judgement about one particular story.

All of it is practitioner craft. None of it is tested evidence, so on the existing doc's scale every entry is "practitioner." No guide here was written for narrated video, and none was tested on engineers. The *For a paper* lines are our application, not the source's.

**The headline finding.** The guides have a name for the form we need, and it is not the full fiction arc. Hart calls it **explanatory narrative** (P2). It is a concrete action line that follows one particular person or thing (P3), broken by short digressions that explain (P2, P4). The guides keep the digressions short (P30), make them answer a question the action just raised (P46), and start them while an outcome is still pending (P44). Our scripts read as lectures because they are mostly digression with a thin action line. Their sentences are also stuck in the middle of the ladder of abstraction (P27), and they hurry the decisive moments while lingering on setup (P28).

## A. Whose story is it? Telling an idea or a discovery as a story

**P1. A story is someone who wants something, meets a complication, and acts on it.**
Jon Franklin's definition, which Hart adopts: a story is "a sequence of actions" that follows when "a sympathetic character encounters a complicating situation." The character confronts the situation and solves it. Hart: "search for the person who makes things happen." Bruce Barcott, quoted by The Open Notebook, puts it as "Somebody wants something."
- *For a paper:* the protagonist is the team, and its want is the paper's stated goal. The complication is the first documented thing that stopped them. A system or a benchmark is not a protagonist.
- *Sources:* Hart, *Storycraft* (2011), via [reader highlights](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/18896512-storycraft/74509229-wally-bock); Aschwanden, ["Naming the Dog"](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2011/09/14/art-of-narrative-structure/), TON, 2011-09-14 (Firecrawl). **Strength:** primary (the book's own words, but through a reader's highlights; Barcott quoted).

**P2. Explanatory narrative: an action line, interrupted by digressions.**
This is Hart's name for the form that fits "the information is the point" subjects. It is "the classic New Yorker form," built from "a narrative line, a sequence of actions with frequent digressions." In the book, the action line "creates its overall shape" and draws readers on with "mild dramatic tension." The digressions "provide the actual explanation." Action sits low on the ladder of abstraction, "where emotion rules," and explanation sits higher. Hart quotes Michael Roberts's name for the result: a "layer cake" of scene and digression.
- *Example:* McPhee riding with a hazmat trucker, in the cab at some points and "off exploring various dimensions of trucking" at others.
- *For a paper:* this, not the full story arc, is the default shape for a paper episode. The action line is the team's sequence of attempts and tests. Each digression explains the piece of mechanism or background that the action has just made necessary.
- *Sources:* Pitzer, ["Jack Hart on Storycraft"](https://niemanstoryboard.org/2011/10/20/jack-hart-storycraft-narrative-nonfiction-interview/), Nieman Storyboard, 2011-10-20 (interview); *Storycraft* via [reader highlights](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/18896512-storycraft/19707788-isaac-butterworth). **Strength:** primary.

**P3. Track one particular person or thing, not a route or a category.**
Hart's coaching note to Rich Read on the Pulitzer-winning "The French Fry Connection": don't just follow a route, "you have to track a person or a thing." Readers must see "particular places at particular times." So Read followed one batch of fries, from one field on one farm, to the packing plant, to the ship, to one McDonald's counter.
- *For a paper:* pick one robot, one design, one episode or one failure case, and carry it through the method and the results. Use the paper's own exemplar wherever one exists. Do not follow "the pipeline" in general.
- *Source:* *Storycraft* via [reader highlights](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/18896512-storycraft/19707788-isaac-butterworth). **Strength:** primary (through highlights).

**P4. The "3+2 explainer": three narrative passages around two digressions.**
Hart's template for a short explanatory narrative:
1. **Narrative 1:** introduce the lead character and pose the explanatory question.
2. **Digression 1:** the background and context.
3. **Narrative 2:** follow the character through the main body of the action line.
4. **Digression 2:** "Complete the explanation."
5. **Narrative 3:** bring the action line "to a logical stopping place."

Two rules go with it: "If you do leave the action line, don't leave for long," and Bill Blundell's rule to "never digress" for more than two paragraphs.
- *For a paper:* this maps onto a chapter, or onto a whole 12-minute episode. N1 is the cold open and the question. D1 is why the problem is hard. N2 is the attempts. D2 is how the method works. N3 is the verdict and what still breaks.
- *Source:* *Storycraft* via [reader highlights](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/18896512-storycraft/19707788-isaac-butterworth). **Strength:** primary (through highlights).

**P5. A flat action line is enough. Narrative is not all-or-nothing.**
Hart says the fries' journey was "a flat narrative line, A to Z, instead of an arc." He adds that "Explanatory narratives often lack something as basic as a protagonist." Siri Carpenter, editor of The Open Notebook: "science stories seldom have a complete narrative thru-line." She recommends "narrative in small doses": anecdotes, scenes "that show how some process unfolds," and snippets of dialogue.
- *For a paper:* don't fake a hero's arc. A clear A-to-Z action line with real stakes and honest small doses of scene is the standard of the form, not a shortfall.
- *Sources:* *Storycraft* via [reader highlights](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/18896512-storycraft/19707788-isaac-butterworth); Carpenter, ["Spotting a Narrative Thread: Resources"](https://www.nasw.org/system/files/articles/Spotting_a_narrative_thread-Resources-Siri_Carpenter.pdf), NASW handout, February 2019 (PDF, direct fetch). **Strength:** primary.

**P6. Follow the researcher's own path to understanding, or an "idea-based chronology."**
Kat McGowan, in "Ask TON": open on a scientist in the middle of a surprising discovery, ask "how did we get here?", then "follow your character's own enlightenment chronologically up to the present moment." With no single character, use "an idea-based chronology," "showing how one question leads to the next." Useful questions for the source: "What didn't quite add up about the explanations you heard?" Mukherjee wrote *The Emperor of All Maladies* in a similar way: each night he wrote until he had "answered the question left over from the night before." His questions were private writing prompts, not lines spoken to the reader.
- *For a paper:* the path is the paper's sequence of questions (baseline, then failure, then a diagnosis, then the fix, then the test), stated in the order the paper or the authors' talk documents.
- *Sources:* ["Ask TON: How to Build Narrative in Explanatory Stories"](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2014/11/11/finding-narrative/), 2014-11-11 (Firecrawl); [4th Estate interview with Mukherjee](https://www.4thestate.co.uk/2011/09/02/interview-with-siddhartha-mukherjee/), 2011-09-02. **Strength:** primary.

**P7. Follow experts through their work, in real places.**
McPhee's usual method is to team up with someone who has a certain expertise, and "you follow them through their work." He describes all his work as "about real people in real places." His geology quartet began when the geologist Ken Deffeyes told him to "go *across* the structure." That advice became a traverse of the continent, with one geologist after another carrying the ideas.
- *For a paper:* if the authors gave a talk, wrote a thread, or published a project page with video, those are the "work" to follow. The lab's own footage of its own runs is the cab of the truck.
- *Source:* ["John McPhee on Characters, Structure, Titles…"](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2011/11/08/john-mcphee/), TON, 2011-11-08, a transcript of a 2011 University of Colorado event (Firecrawl). **Strength:** primary.

**P8. The idea or the problem can be the protagonist, but it gets no feelings.**
Shi En Kim, writing for TON: "make the idea itself a character." The science writer Emily Willingham adds that, as with any character, "there needs to be some inciting incident." Kim praises an account of a wolf, O-Six, built from watchers' logs "without falling into the trap of speculatively assigning motives." Her rule is to keep to the facts and avoid anthropomorphizing non-human characters.
- *For a paper:* the problem (say, the sim-to-real gap) can be the thread that the whole episode follows. The robot is described only by its logged behaviour.
- *Source:* Kim, ["Choosing Unconventional Main Characters"](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2023/01/31/choosing-unconventional-main-characters/), TON, 2023-01-31 (Firecrawl). **Strength:** secondary (Kim describing others' work), with primary quotes.

**P9. Build on the documented tension, not on the news hook.**
Natalie Wolchover (Quanta): "A story has a tension and a resolution." For LIGO's detection, she did not frame the story around what we would learn about black holes, which was still unresolved. She framed it around the century of doubt that gravitational waves could be detected at all. That doubt was the tension, and it justified explaining the machine. Ed Yong, on his profile of a scientific dynasty: "This conflict really gave a center to the piece." Without it, he says, the piece risked being list-like: "Here's a guy, and here's another guy."
- *For a paper:* the tension is whatever the paper resolves, stated as a doubt the field held. It comes from the introduction or related work, never from our own framing.
- *Sources:* Hall, ["An Interview with Natalie Wolchover"](https://showcase.casw.org/?p=534), CASW Showcase, 2016-09-06; Zimmer, ["Ed Yong Profiles a Scientific Dynasty"](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2013/02/19/ed-yong-profiles-a-scientific-dynasty/), TON, 2013-02-19 (Firecrawl). **Strength:** primary.

**P10. Frame a hard topic as a mystery, then answer it without hype.**
Clark: "You can raise questions for readers that the story will answer," framed "as secrets to be exposed or mysteries to be solved." He adds: "You can do this without hype. No click-baiting allowed." He also passes on a rule: never raise a question "that you are unprepared to answer." David Quammen, on epidemics: "it's all about mystery stories… these people are the detectives."
- *Example:* David Leonhardt's newsletter "An epidemiological whodunnit." It asked why Covid's toll was low across much of Africa and Asia, then ended without "ramming home" a single solution: "The full answer to this mystery surely involves multiple explanations."
- *For a paper:* this goes beyond "open with a mystery" (existing doc F6, C1). The mystery must be one the paper actually answers. When the answer has several parts, the ending should say so.
- *Sources:* Clark, ["When the topic is tough, frame it as a 'mystery' to be solved"](https://poynter.org/reporting-editing/2021/when-the-topic-is-tough-frame-it-as-a-mystery-to-be-solved/), Poynter, 2021-03-09; Dobbs, ["David Quammen on Turning Research Into Story"](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2013/01/22/david-quammen-on-turning-research-into-story-part-1/), TON, 2013-01-22 (Firecrawl). **Strength:** primary.

**P11. Find the action inside the paper.**
Marion Renault (TON): a paper's methods section "will (by definition) describe a sequence of events." In her example, "exposed to auditory stimuli" meant that someone clapped beside the mice. Writers she quotes ask the researchers when the story of the work began, what the dead ends were, and what "the biggest hurdles" were.
- *For a paper:* rewrite each method step as who did what to what, with which result ("the team filmed each robot up to three times"). Ask the appendix and the talk for dead ends.
- *Source:* Renault, ["How to Cultivate Narrative in Stories of All Lengths"](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2022/11/22/how-to-cultivate-narrative-in-stories-of-all-lengths/), TON, 2022-11-22 (Firecrawl). **Strength:** primary.

**P12. Decisions drive the chain: each action should trigger the next.**
Will Storr: "one action from a character should trigger the next action." There should be "as few as possible incidents" that happen "just because they have to happen." In science communication, "the most effective communication is rigorous about cause and effect." His counter-example, from a class he teaches, is the opening of *Twin Peaks* series 3. It has no cause and effect, and viewers went from confused to irritated to laughing. "That's how you lose an audience." A review of the book sums up its opening principle: "All good stories start with change, or the promise of change."
- *For a paper:* this sharpens the existing doc's but/therefore rule (F1): the *therefore* should be a decision the team made because of the last result ("because none of the 15 crossed, they asked for bench measurements"). If the paper doesn't record the "because," say "the paper next reports," not "so they."
- *Sources:* Penn, ["Character Flaws with Will Storr"](https://www.thecreativepenn.com/2020/05/25/character-flaws-will-storr/), The Creative Penn, 2020-05-25 (interview); Sutton, ["Storytelling is your best weapon for convincing people"](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/storytelling-your-best-weapon-convincing-people), *The Psychologist*, 2021-12-06 (interview); Brookman-Byrne, ["The secret of great storytelling"](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/secret-great-storytelling), *The Psychologist*, 2019-09-30 (review). **Strength:** primary (interviews); secondary (review).

**P13. The dramatic question: the plot tests one precisely defined flaw. In a paper, the flaw belongs to an idea, not a person.**
Storr: "The fundamental purpose of story is to work out who is this character?" "Your plot is designed specifically to plot against your character." He defines the flaw "with absolute precision." His example is *Jaws*: Brody fears the water, the shark forces him into it, and the last scene has him swimming back. He also says: "I don't think transformation is a prerequisite of storytelling."
- *For a paper:* we can't diagnose anyone's psychology, and we never impute motives. What can carry the "flaw" is a documented belief the paper tests, named in its introduction or related work (for example, "a simulator built from public part data is enough"). The experiments are the plot against that belief, and the change is the belief revised.
- *Source:* Penn 2020 (interview). **Strength:** primary.

## B. Arc, complication, and the shapes that carry explanation

**P14. The narrative arc, phase by phase, and what each phase is for.**
Hart's five phases:
1. **Exposition.** It "introduce[s] a protagonist," with "just enough" backstory.
2. **Complication.** The inciting incident, or "Plot Point A," which "rips the protagonist out of the status quo."
3. **Rising action.** The protagonist "grapples with the complication." This phase carries most of the bulk.
4. **Crisis.** "the point where everything hangs in the balance."
5. **Climax and resolution.** The climax "resolves the crisis." It is reached through a "point of insight, in which the protagonist sees the world in the new way."

After the climax comes the falling action, or denouement. Hart's caveats: "You don't always have all those elements in a nonfiction narrative," and "Your plot… need not follow the narrative arc."
- *For a paper:* exposition is the goal, the setting and the terms. The complication is the first documented failure. Rising action is the attempts and fixes. The crisis is the test that could go either way. The climax is the verdict. The falling action is the limits.
- *Sources:* Pitzer 2011 (interview); *Storycraft* via [reader highlights](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/18896512-storycraft/74509229-wally-bock). **Strength:** primary.

**P15. Exposition is "the enemy of narrative": keep it minimal and inside the action.**
Hart: "write no more than absolutely necessary." "You launch action immediately and then blend the exposition into it," submerged in clauses and appositives. Bill Blundell calls this the "sandwich technique": "Action's the bread. Exposition's the filling." Hart also says what exposition is for: "The only necessary exposition serves dramatic purposes." It explains why the protagonist must face the complication, or "why the challenge is so daunting."
- *For a paper:* background earns its place only if it explains why the obstacle is hard. Background that doesn't (lab history, a general survey of the field) is cut.
- *Source:* *Storycraft* via reader highlights ([1](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/18896512-storycraft/74509229-wally-bock), [2](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/18896512-storycraft/19707788-isaac-butterworth)). **Strength:** primary.

**P16. Rising action is the rise and fall of hope. Every scene should move the protagonist's position.**
Hart: "Batman wins one. Hope rises. The Joker wins one. Hope falls." He sketches this as a curve writhing up a curve, with each plot point marked. He also cites:
- **Robert McKee:** each scene must change the protagonist's "value charge," whether they are up or down relative to the goal.
- **Peter Rubie:** a good scene should "cause a subsequent scene to occur." It should be "driven by the main character's needs," and include action that changes the character's position relative to the end.
- **Hart:** "Drama is interesting only when opposing forces appear evenly matched."
- *For a paper:* label each chapter + or − (the result helped, or the result exposed a new problem). A run of chapters that are all + is a lecture. The existing doc's try-fail cycles (F4) are one instance of this.
- *Source:* *Storycraft* via [reader highlights](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/18896512-storycraft/19707788-isaac-butterworth). **Strength:** primary (Hart), quoting others.

**P17. The climax turns on a point of insight. Find the event that sets it up.**
Hart: even without "a true psychological transformation," it is "useful to think of the crisis culminating in a point of insight." His working question: "What event sets up the resolution of the crisis?"
- *For a paper:* the insight is usually a sourced diagnosis, such as an ablation, a failure analysis, or the authors' own statement of what mattered. The event that sets it up is the experiment that produced that diagnosis. Show the event, then give the insight.
- *Source:* *Storycraft* via [reader highlights](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/18896512-storycraft/19707788-isaac-butterworth). **Strength:** primary.

**P18. "Repeated Attempts": return again and again to the point of tension.**
Michelle Nijhuis names the structure of McPhee's "Atchafalaya" this way: "I'd call it Repeated Attempts." The piece keeps returning to Old River Control, the Army Corps of Engineers' effort to stop the Mississippi from changing course. When Pamela Weintraub had "no natural through-story," she hung the research on a second narrative. Nijhuis: "you don't have to write your story as you reported it."
- *For a paper:* many engineering papers are repeated attempts at one obstacle (one failure mode attacked by a baseline, then a fix, then a second fix). Return to the same failing case each time, so the viewer can measure progress against it.
- *Source:* Rosen, ["Narrative X-Rays"](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2015/10/20/narrative-x-rays-stories-structural-skeletons/), TON, 2015-10-20 (Firecrawl). **Strength:** primary (Nijhuis, a practitioner, analysing McPhee).

**P19. The more complex the material, the simpler the structure. Use chronology unless it misleads.**
Tom French: "The more complex the material, the simpler the structure should be." McPhee, on a journey piece that he structured by theme: "a chronological structure would be misleading." The reason: "Things happened… And they happened in themes." Carl Zimmer allows jumps in time, but readers "need to see why you make every jump."
- *For a paper:* default to the order of the questions. Switch to a thematic order (for example, by failure mode) only when the paper's results are organised that way, and say so on screen.
- *Sources:* Aschwanden 2011, TON (Firecrawl); McPhee, "Structure," *The New Yorker*, 2013-01-14, as quoted in ["On Narrative Structure: Denby v. McPhee"](https://thenewyorkerandme.blogspot.com/2023/04/on-narrative-structure-denby-v-mcphee.html), blog, 2023-04-16 (the original is paywalled and was not read); Zimmer, ["Science Writing: Guidelines and Guidance"](https://carlzimmer.com/science-writing-guidelines-and-guidance/), undated class notes. **Strength:** primary (McPhee's words quoted by a third party).

**P20. Each section gets its own small arc and ends on the next tension.**
Wolchover writes in sections of about 800 words, "each one with its own tension-resolution arc." Each ends by hinting at the next tension "so the reader can't bail." She is always "holding something back." Clark's quick list: "To propel readers, make them wait." Hart wrote the cliff-hanger under each scene box of his plan: "The deckhand pushing off the dock."
- *For a paper:* each chapter's last line should name the next documented problem or open question ("but a third still failed"). It should never be a summary of the chapter just finished.
- *Sources:* Hall 2016, CASW; Clark, ["Fifty Writing Tools: Quick List"](https://poynter.org/?p=76067), Poynter, 2006-06-30; *Storycraft* via reader highlights. **Strength:** primary.

**P21. The bookend: hold a dense middle between two scenes.**
Hart's "bookend narrative" brackets "a stretch of expository material with two pieces of more engaging scenic action." The opening and closing scenes have "the power to hold the longer, duller content in the center."
- *For a paper:* cold open on the decisive real clip, explain the method, then close on the same clip with the answer. This is the existing doc's bookend, with Hart's reason for it: the scenes are what carry the dull middle.
- *Source:* *Storycraft* via [reader highlights](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/18896512-storycraft/19707788-isaac-butterworth). **Strength:** primary.

**P22. OCAR and its faster cousins: decide how long to hold the answer.**
Joshua Schimel's OCAR, from his own blog:
- **Opening:** "should identify the larger problem you are contributing to."
- **Challenge:** "there is often one overarching question, which others flesh out."
- **Action:** the key results, "no more than 2-3 points."
- **Resolution:** "If readers remember only one thing from your work, this should be it."

In the book (read through a lecture's slides and a reader's quote sheet), he ranks structures by speed. OCAR is the slowest. ABDCE (Anne Lamott's formula) opens on action. LDR, "Lead, Development, Resolution," is "Faster yet – but people will read to the end." LD puts "the whole story… up front." Within a piece, most paragraphs are point-first. The point-last ones go "at critical story transition points," making "a series of nested arcs from sentences to the whole paper."
- *For a paper:* treat the episode as LDR: hook with the anomaly, develop, and resolve at the verdict. Make every explanation paragraph point-first, and keep point-last only for the turns (the reversal, the verdict). This reconciles suspense with what experts want.
- *Sources:* Schimel, ["Writing Science Getting Started Group Exercise"](https://schimelwritingscience.wordpress.com/2021/10/06/writing-science-getting-started-group-exercise/), 2021-10-06 (direct fetch); Xiao, [slides on *Writing Science*](https://yncenter.sites.yale.edu/sites/default/files/xiao_wei_jun_2012.pdf), Yale, 2012-06-15; Burgin, ["Schimel in a sheet"](https://burginlab.files.wordpress.com/2016/07/schimel-in-a-sheet-copy.pdf), 2016. **Strength:** primary (OCAR); secondary (the speeds and the nested arcs).

**P23. One problem per story: Olson's Narrative Spectrum.**
Randy Olson's spectrum runs from AAA to DHY, with ABT in the middle:
- **AAA** ("and, and, and"): "you're still going to bore the listener."
- **DHY** ("despite, however, yet"): "you're still going to confuse the listener."

"Adding a second word of contradiction… suddenly establishes a second narrative." His example is a campaign to save 50 acres of woods. Adding that the developer is a convicted felon creates two problems, so one should go.
- *For a paper:* give the episode one central BUT, the paper's core problem. Sub-obstacles nest under it as steps. A second, unrelated problem (a side result) becomes a short aside or is cut.
- *Sources:* Olson, *The Narrative Gym* (2020), chapters 4–5. This is a full-text PDF on co-author Park Howell's [businessofstory.com](https://businessofstory.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/THE-NARRATIVE-GYM-BOOK.pdf); its authorisation is not confirmed, so check the quotes against a bought copy. Also Dennison, [review of *Houston, We Have a Narrative*](https://ian.umces.edu/blog/randy-olsons-new-book-houston-we-have-a-narrative/), UMCES IAN, 2016-01-05. **Strength:** primary (book text, provenance flagged); secondary (review).

**P24. The Pixar "story basics" that survive in nonfiction.**
Emma Coats's 22 #storybasics are her own list, "a mix of things learned from directors & coworkers at Pixar," not official Pixar rules. Four transfer:
- **#1:** "You admire a character for trying more than for their successes."
- **#19:** "coincidences to get them out of it are cheating."
- **#16:** "What happens if they don't succeed?" In nonfiction, only stakes the sources state count.
- **#13:** "Give your characters opinions." For a paper, these are only the authors' own stated claims.

Pixar in a Box (Khan Academy) defines the antagonist as "a force that gets in the way of your character's wants and needs." Marlin's antagonist is "the ocean and his fear of it." So the opponent can be the physical problem rather than a villain. **Excluded:** #5's "Combine characters" (that makes composites) and any stacking of unsourced odds.
- *For a paper:* show the attempts as well as the win. Every way out must be causal: a fix found in the data, not luck. The antagonist is the physics or the gap.
- *Sources:* Coats, ["22 #storybasics I've picked up in my time at Pixar"](https://web.archive.org/web/20130626/https://storyshots.tumblr.com/post/25032057278/22-storybasics-ive-picked-up-in-my-time-at-pixar), June 2012 (archive.org snapshot from 2013-06-26, read with Firecrawl); Maschwitz, prolost.com, 2013-12-04 (on the list's provenance); Khan Academy, *Pixar in a Box*, "Act 1" (undated). **Strength:** primary.

## C. Scene and summary: when to zoom in

**P25. Reports give information, stories give experience. Choose per passage.**
Clark's quick list: use "one to render information, the other to render experience." Reports answer who, what, where, when and why. Stories use "Characters, Scenes, Settings, Chronology, Motives." Hart: "most of the topics they tackle are inappropriate for a narrative approach," and sometimes "It's the information that's important, not the story itself."
- *For a paper:* the numbers, definitions and caveats are report passages. Write them as plainly as a report, inside a story frame. Story structure is not a licence to make the numbers less direct.
- *Sources:* Clark, ["Fifty Writing Tools: Quick List"](https://poynter.org/?p=76067), 2006-06-30; Clark, ["A whimsical request inspired some essential writing tools"](https://niemanstoryboard.org/?p=2384), Nieman Storyboard, 2023-02-07; Pitzer 2011. **Strength:** primary.

**P26. Summary and scene are two distances from the action.**
Hart: "When the distance is great… you write in summary narrative." When you move closer, you "shift into scenic narrative." The difference "lies in the relative positions of the two forms on the ladder of abstraction." Summary collapses time and spans categories. Scene puts the reader "into a scene where they can experience the action." Hart rejects "show, don't tell." Good writers "show and tell."
- *For a paper:* summary covers the 13,000 builds. Scene covers the one robot whose gear walked out of mesh on take 2. Both are needed, and the script should mark which one each paragraph is.
- *Source:* *Storycraft* via [reader highlights](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/18896512-storycraft/19707788-isaac-butterworth). **Strength:** primary.

**P27. Climb up and down the ladder of abstraction, and beware getting stuck in the middle.**
Clark: at the bottom are "bloody knives and rosary beads," and at the top are words "that reach for a higher meaning." "Good writers move up and down the ladder." "Beware of the middle," where reporters get "trapped halfway up." Clark's workshop test: summarise the story in three words or fewer to find what it is "really about." The Open Notebook's science version: select "a single abstract takeaway" for the top. The middle rungs are "where writers often have to do the most explanatory writing," so they must be "sturdy."
- *Example:* Updike's beer can. "Pop-top" is at the bottom of the ladder, and "aesthetic experience" is at the top.
- *For a paper:* each chapter should touch the bottom (one clip, one number, one part), the top (the one-sentence insight), and the mechanism in between. A chapter that stays in "the framework leverages…" is stuck in the middle.
- *Sources:* Clark, ["Writing Tool #13: Show and Tell"](https://poynter.org/?p=23159), Poynter, 2004-05-19; Madhusoodanan, ["Using the Ladder of Abstraction to Elevate Science Stories"](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2023/05/30/using-the-ladder-of-abstraction-to-elevate-science-stories/), TON, 2023-05-30 (Firecrawl). **Strength:** primary (Clark); secondary (TON applying Clark).

**P28. The pace paradox: hurry through the routine, slow down at the best moments.**
Tom French, quoted by Hart: "when you're in the boring stuff, that's when you need to speed up," and "when you're in the best stuff… you slow down." Slowing down lets the reader "really enter that scene," with "that delicious sense of enforced waiting." Hart also quotes French on "the power and importance of tiny, tiny moments."
- *For a paper:* this is the rule for when to zoom in. Spend seconds on setup boilerplate and a full beat (slow clip, the number built up on screen) on the decisive run, the reversal and the verdict. Most scripts do the opposite.
- *Source:* *Storycraft* via reader highlights ([1](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/18896512-storycraft/74509229-wally-bock), [2](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/18896512-storycraft/19707788-isaac-butterworth)). **Strength:** primary (French, quoted by Hart).

**P29. Make the problem one sudden, singular moment.**
Olson: "Narrative is at its most powerful with 'the number one.'" Also: "The more singular and instantaneous the moment, the more powerful." His example is a 40-storey building. Demolished in seconds, it makes a strong story. Taken apart over three years, it doesn't, unless a time-lapse compresses it into one moment.
- *For a paper:* find the single moment that *is* the problem (the take where the tyre slips) and the single moment that shows the fix working. Compress slow processes, such as training curves or a thousand simulations, into one time-lapse beat.
- *Source:* Olson, *The Narrative Gym*, chapter 6 (provenance flagged in P23). **Strength:** primary.

**P30. How much scene, and how long away from it.**
The guides give different numbers:
- **Gutkind's "yellow test":** highlight every place where "characters are *doing things*." His claim: "You will find you have highlighted a major portion of the text."
- **Hart:** about 500 words minimum for a full scene.
- **Nijhuis:** even "a couple sentences" of scene "can do a lot."
- **Anil Ananthaswamy:** "no more that 3-4 paragraphs of physics" before returning to the place or the people.
- **Blundell:** at most two paragraphs away from the narrative.
- **Renault:** a 500-word piece has room for "one vivid scene, one moment of tension."
- *For a paper:* a workable rule from these is a return to something concrete (a clip, a run, a named result) at least every 3–4 explanation beats.
- *Sources:* Gutkind, "The Yellow Test," *NYT Opinionator*, 2012-08-27, as quoted by [Publication Coach](https://www.publicationcoach.com/?p=3501) (the original is paywalled and was not read); Selby, ["Writing Elegant Background"](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2016/03/29/writing-elegant-background/), TON, 2016-03-29; Ananthaswamy, ["Weaving Exposition into Narrative"](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2014/04/22/weaving-exposition-into-narrative/), TON, 2014-04-22; Renault 2022; *Storycraft* via highlights. **Strength:** primary (Ananthaswamy, Nijhuis, Renault, Hart); secondary (Gutkind).

**P31. Every scene has to earn its place: the cut test.**
Zimmer: "Before you add a scene to a story, make sure it matters." Cut it and see: if the story still holds, "the scene is a digression," and "it has to go." Also: "When you open a scene, set it." Hart: "every detail must advance the action line." He quotes Elmore Leonard: "I try to leave out the parts that people skip." McPhee: "Writing is selection… I left out more than I put down."
- *For a paper:* delete each clip or example in turn. If the explanation of the mechanism or the verdict still holds, the clip is decoration.
- *Sources:* Zimmer, [class notes](https://carlzimmer.com/science-writing-guidelines-and-guidance/) (direct fetch); *Storycraft* via highlights; McPhee, *Draft No. 4*, as quoted by Clark, ["If you want to write a book, John McPhee's advice is indispensable"](https://www.poynter.org/?p=497750), Poynter, 2018-01-10. **Strength:** primary.

**P32. Give a number a face, and keep its denominator.**
María Paula Rubiano A., quoted by Renault: a named case "helps a reader understand the depth of the numbers." Renault's 500-word budget keeps "one fleshed-out character" for the same job.
- *For a paper:* every headline number gets one concrete instance (the clip of one of the 15 failures). The instance is labelled as one of N, never as typical (existing doc N1, N10).
- *Source:* Renault 2022, TON (quoting Rubiano). **Strength:** primary.

**P33. Point of view: whose eyes, from which direction, at what distance.**
Hart reduces point of view to three questions: "Through whose eyes do we experience the story? From which direction? From what distance?" The "dramatic point of view," the movie-camera approach, "sticks to an external reality that can be verified." That makes it "the most objective and journalistic" option. Hart's "hanging-balloon" view watches from "just a few feet away." Clark's quick list: "Turn your notebook into a 'camera.'"
- *For a paper:* the faceless channel's default should be the dramatic, camera point of view. Show what the robot did and what the logs record, from a fixed vantage. Interior views are only possible through the authors' own published words.
- *Sources:* *Storycraft* via [reader highlights](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/18896512-storycraft/74509229-wally-bock); Clark, Quick List, 2006. **Strength:** primary.

**P34. Stay close to the action. Don't drag in an expert to explain.**
Tom French, quoted by Hart, warns against extended flashbacks, ornate flash-forwards and "the so-called expert dragged forward" to explain. "Stay as close to the action as possible."
- *For a paper:* explain through what the system did on screen. Don't cut away to a generic explainer section that has lost touch with the robot.
- *Source:* *Storycraft* via [reader highlights](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/18896512-storycraft/74509229-wally-bock). **Strength:** primary (French, quoted by Hart).

**P35. Wolfe's four devices, and which of them survive in an explainer.**
In the introduction to *The New Journalism* (1973), Tom Wolfe named four devices:
1. Scene-by-scene construction: moving from scene to scene, with as little historical summary as possible.
2. Dialogue recorded in full.
3. Third-person point of view, presenting scenes through a character's eyes.
4. "Status details," the symbols of a character's status and way of life.

Hart calls scene-by-scene construction the technique "that most distinguishes narrative nonfiction from expository forms." Kallan's essay adds, citing the book, that Wolfe aimed to convey "the subjective and emotional life of the characters."
- *For a paper:* devices 1 and 2 transfer, as scenes and the authors' own quoted words. Device 4 becomes the telling physical detail (the LEGO-compatible parts, the 44 tries). Device 3, the interior view, does not transfer without the person's own statement (see P36).
- *Sources:* Kallan, essay on Wolfe, [UPenn course page](https://web.english.upenn.edu/~despey/wolfe.htm) (undated); [Wikipedia, "The New Journalism"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Journalism); *Storycraft* via highlights. Wolfe's own text was not reached: no excerpt was found, and Rolling Stone redirected to a pay-per-crawl gate, which was not used. **Strength:** secondary.

## D. Character without invention

**P36. The accuracy covenant: what a true story may never do.**
Mark Kramer's "Breakable Rules" (rule 2) says literary journalists "work out implicit covenants about accuracy and candor." The conventions he lists:
- "no composite scenes, no misstated chronology"
- "no invention of quotes"
- no attribution of thoughts to sources unless "the sources have said they'd had those very thoughts"

Walt Harrington, quoted by Hart, won't say what someone is thinking "unless they tell you what they're thinking." Hart: "If you are changing or inventing facts… you are writing fiction." The real route is "good faith": "you never deliberately falsify anything, large or small."
- *For a paper:* this is the craft tradition behind our "never allowed" list (existing doc section 4), and it is stricter than some guides (see the conflicts below). The only window into the team's thinking is what they wrote or said, quoted and dated.
- *Sources:* Kramer, ["Breakable Rules for Literary Journalists"](https://niemanstoryboard.org/stories/breakable-rules-for-literary-journalists/), Nieman Storyboard, 1995-01-01; *Storycraft* via highlights. **Strength:** primary.

**P37. Tell the listener how you know: reconstruct, attribute, cross-check.**
Zimmer: "we don't make up scenes. We reconstruct them from our reporting." Hart: "readers should know exactly where you learned what you claim to know." Tom Hallman attributed internal monologue to the source's memory, with phrases like "he recalled thinking." Quammen makes clear that a scene "as described is Brian Amman's account." Rubiano: when sources disagree, "omit the detail or explicitly state the difference." Hart: "Reconstructed dialogue is especially suspect." When unsure of the exact words, paraphrase instead.
- *For a paper:* spoken attributions do this work and cost two seconds: "the paper reports," "in the authors' video," "the appendix says." So does the on-screen source chip.
- *Sources:* Zimmer, class notes; *Storycraft* via highlights; Dobbs 2013 (Quammen); Rubiano A., ["Writing Vivid Scenes from a Distance"](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2021/06/08/writing-vivid-scenes-from-a-distance-lessons-from-audio-producers/), TON, 2021-06-08 (Firecrawl). **Strength:** primary.

**P38. Show character through action, and give studies human subjects.**
Zimmer: "Demonstrating a person's brilliance or passion **in action** will make a far deeper impression." Also: "Studies don't do anything. People run studies." Yong, on sources calling his subject "supportive": "that's telling me, not showing me." Clark's quick list: "Show characteristics through scenes, details, and dialogue." Hart: "nothing reveals character like an anecdote."
- *For a paper:* show what the team chose to do, from the paper's record. "They built every simulated pass by hand, about 13,000 pieces, and filmed each up to three times" characterises them without a single adjective. Never call them "meticulous."
- *Sources:* Zimmer, class notes; Zimmer 2013 (Yong); Clark, Quick List; *Storycraft* via highlights. **Strength:** primary.

**P39. Choose subjects who act, not subjects things happen to.**
Hart: "A good protagonist is, most of all, the captain of his fate." He adds that journalists tend to focus on victims and perpetrators, "neither one of which make terribly good protagonists." *This American Life* producer Nadia Reiman, in Nieman Storyboard, warns that otherwise people "become characters that something happens to." Kim warns against anthropomorphising non-human characters.
- *For a paper:* the team acts (designs, tests, diagnoses), and the problem resists. The robot and the model are instruments. They "slip," "fail," or "score," but they never "want," "try" or "learn" in the human sense.
- *Sources:* *Storycraft* via highlights; Pitzer 2011; Armstrong, ["Ira Glass on 30 years of This American Life"](https://niemanstoryboard.org/2025/05/16/ira-glass-storytelling-lessons-30-years-this-american-life/), Nieman Storyboard, 2025-05-16; Kim 2023. **Strength:** primary (Hart, Reiman); secondary (Kim).

**P40. Let the people tell it in their own words. Dialogue is action.**
Jacqui Banaszynski, via TON: "Turn your sources into the storytellers." Emily Mullin: "Get your source to tell a story, then use that story in yours." Clark's quick list: "Dialogue advances narrative; quotes delay it." Don Murray, quoted by Hart: "dialogue is not talk but action."
- *For a paper:* the authors' own sentences from the paper, the talk or the thread are our only "dialogue." Use the ones that do something (state a decision, a surprise, a doubt). Skip the ones that only praise.
- *Sources:* "Ask TON" 2014; Renault 2022; Clark, Quick List; *Storycraft* via highlights. **Strength:** primary.

**P41. No "casting": keep the grey, and let the reader judge.**
Hart: you cannot "seek out sources you know will make your point" and ignore the ones that would muddy your theme. He calls that "casting." McPhee, on *Encounters with the Archdruid*: "I wanted the gray, in all shades, to be present." He "hoped that readers would enter the scenes… and come to their own conclusions." He usually lets judgement "be in the conclusion of the reader."
- *For a paper:* include the result that complicates the story (the level-3 drop, the run-to-run variance) in the chapter where it belongs, not in a footnote. Leave the verdict on significance to the sourced reactions.
- *Sources:* *Storycraft* via highlights; McPhee, TON 2011 (Firecrawl). **Strength:** primary.

## E. Curiosity and suspense through structure

**P42. Give the story an engine: one key question the action answers.**
Clark's tool 31: "Good stories need an engine, a question the action answers for the reader." Tom French calls it the "engine" that drives the narrative "until the reader's curiosity is satisfied." Darin Strauss, quoted by Hart, draws each plot line as an arc from A to B: "A is to be a question; B is its answer." The question should relate to "the concrete desire of the protagonist." Schimel, in the book as summarised in a lecture's slides, says the same in science terms. Write "Our question was," not "Our objectives were," because objectives raise no curiosity.
- *For a paper:* one engine question per episode, stated in the cold open and answered in the verdict. Each chapter gets a smaller A→B question nested inside it. The team's goal is restated as the question it implies.
- *Sources:* Clark, Quick List, 2006; Clark, ["The power of story engines"](https://www.poynter.org/news/power-story-engines), Poynter, 2007-04-23; *Storycraft* via highlights; Xiao 2012 (Schimel). **Strength:** primary (Clark, Strauss); secondary (Schimel).

**P43. Little mysteries and subtle foreshadowing in the setup.**
Hart: "little mysteries drive the narrative forward." Blundell, quoted by Hart: "tease the folks a little bit in the lead." Hart says foreshadowing belongs in the exposition, done subtly. A writer who notes that a character doesn't expect the worst tells readers she's about to get it. Clark's quick list: "Plant important clues early."
- *For a paper:* plant the detail that will matter later. "Note the gear train; it comes back" is crude. Showing the gear in the first clip is better.
- *Sources:* *Storycraft* via [reader highlights](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/18896512-storycraft/74509229-wally-bock); Clark, Quick List. **Strength:** primary.

**P44. Digress in the middle of the action, not between actions.**
Kramer: "Good storytellers often digress at moments when especially interesting action is pending." He adds: "When the author drops you back… the place feels familiar." Hart: "bailing out of the action line at just the right moment" preserves tension. He quotes Kramer: "It's frequently best to digress in the middle of the action."
- *For a paper:* stop the clip just before the outcome (the robot at the step), explain the mechanism that decides it, then play the outcome. Don't finish the clip and then explain.
- *Sources:* Kramer 1995; *Storycraft* via highlights. **Strength:** primary.

**P45. Make the promise early, but hold something back.**
Zimmer: after the nut graf, "you have now made a promise to your readers." He quotes the radio producer Nancy Updike: telling readers where a story is going "doesn't spoil the ending for them. It primes them for it." McPhee, as quoted by Clark: "A lead is a promise." The lead is "a flashlight that shines down into the story." Wolchover's intro must "deliver the main news or takeaway… without leaving them fully satisfied." Her second section opens: "OK, let's back up for a second."
- *For a paper:* the cold open states the question and the headline outcome, then withholds the how and the cost. Withholding the headline itself past its chapter is not allowed (existing doc section 4).
- *Sources:* Zimmer, class notes; Clark 2018 (McPhee); Hall 2016 (Wolchover). **Strength:** primary.

**P46. Give background only when the listener has the question.**
Banaszynski: background should "answer, in brief, the questions that the reader has" at the moment the writer introduces something new. Answer them "right then," rather than "dumping background in a section in the middle." Wolchover compares a story to a Jenga tower: pull out "everything you don't need" and "convey details in passing." Hart: skilled writers slip "backstory into main clauses."
- *For a paper:* this is the craft form of "science on demand" (existing doc C1). Introduce each term in the sentence where the viewer first needs it. A glossary chapter is the failure case.
- *Sources:* Selby 2016, TON; Musser, ["Storygram: Natalie Wolchover's 'Visions of Future Physics'"](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2017/02/28/storygram-natalie-wolchovers-visions-of-future-physics/), TON, 2017-02-28; *Storycraft* via highlights. **Strength:** primary.

**P47. Make tension out of the real uncertainty: false alarms, reversals, a late reveal.**
In a TON Storygram of Andrew Grant's Voyager story, the annotators note this line: "Stone and his colleagues resisted that conclusion." It reverses the reader's expectation and so "triggers curiosity." A detail held back, that the plasma instrument had stopped working decades earlier, "maintains narrative tension." David Dobbs, interviewing Quammen, says research puzzlement is "more true to the progress of science than a book of forced epiphanies."
- *For a paper:* the documented false starts (an early result that looked better than it was, a metric that misled) are the honest suspense. Reveal each constraint at the moment it bites.
- *Sources:* Holloway et al., ["Storygram: Andrew Grant's 'At Last, Voyager 1 Slips into Interstellar Space'"](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2017/12/12/storygram-andrew-grants-at-last-voyager-1-slips-into-interstellar-space/), TON, 2017-12-12; Dobbs 2013. **Strength:** opinion (the annotators), with a worked example.

**P48. Place gold coins along the path, especially in the middle.**
Clark: "Don't load all your best stuff high in the story." He says to reward the reader "especially in the middle." The coins are "anecdotes, examples, good quotes, interesting facts." Mary Roach hands readers "gifts" (surprising facts) to keep them reading.
- *For a paper:* plan two or three sourced surprises for the middle third: a striking clip, a counter-intuitive ablation, a candid line from the authors. Don't spend them all in the cold open.
- *Sources:* Clark, ["If I Were a Carpenter"](https://poynter.org/?p=1812), Poynter, 2002-08-14; Clark 2023, Nieman Storyboard; [Missouri Review interview with Mary Roach](https://missourireview.com/?p=15158), 2015-01-12. **Strength:** primary.

**P49. Alternate plot and ideas. Any sequence of actions creates a question.**
Ira Glass: "there's plot and then there are ideas." Plot is "a series of actions where one thing leads to the next." It "creates narrative suspense because you wonder what happened next." Glass, paraphrasing Barthes: any sequence of actions makes the listener ask where it is going. His go-to question for subjects, borrowed from Noah Adams: "how did you think it was going to work out before it happened?"
- *For a paper:* an action beat ("they sent the failure video back to the agent") is followed by an idea beat (why that might work). An idea beat with no action before it is lecture.
- *Source:* ["Q&A: Ira Glass"](https://www.cjr.org/special_report/qa-ira-glass-turnaround-npr-jesse-thorn-tal.php), *CJR*, 2017-06-22; Nieman Storyboard 2025. **Strength:** primary (audio narrative, not prose).

**P50. Start with one person in one situation, and hold the real complexity back.**
Storr: "You begin in the most simple place possible," "hopefully with one person," in a situation. Then "wait, I would say even until the third quarter, to add the real complexity." On Malcolm Gladwell's ketchup piece, a "very simple puzzle," he says: "he builds it into this absolute mystery."
- *For a paper:* open on one robot and one failure. Bring in the variants, the levels and the per-agent tables later. Keep nuance such as variance and edge cases for the last third, but never later than the chapter whose claim depends on it.
- *Source:* Sutton 2021, *The Psychologist* (interview). **Strength:** primary.

## F. Setups, payoffs and endings

**P51. Anything set up must pay off, and a setup must not mislead.**
Hart: "Just mention the paperweight and the implication is that it will play a part." Readers expect Chekhov's rule, which Hart quotes: "One must not put a loaded rifle on the stage" if no one will fire it. George Musser's TON critique of one profile: it "led the reader to expect a trifling response" to critics. That is a setup the story did not honour.
- *For a paper:* every term defined, clip shown or number planted in the first third must be used later. Cut it or use it. Never frame a criticism as minor and then show it was not.
- *Sources:* *Storycraft* via highlights; Musser 2017, TON. **Strength:** primary.

**P52. Know the ending before drafting.**
McPhee, as quoted by Clark: "I always know where I intend to end," before he has much begun to write. Clark's endings advice includes "Try writing your ending first" and "Think of the story as a journey and the ending as a destination." Hart says Franklin suggests "finding your way into stories from the resolution end." Coats's Pixar list, #7: "Come up with your ending before you figure out your middle."
- *For a paper:* write the last 60 seconds first (the answer, the remaining limit, the open question), then check that every chapter leads there.
- *Sources:* Clark 2018 (McPhee); Krueger, ["4 strategies for writing story endings"](https://www.poynter.org/educators-students/2017/4-strategies-for-writing-story-endings/), Poynter, 2017-04-05 (from Clark's *Help! for Writers*); *Storycraft* via highlights. **Strength:** primary.

**P53. The ending echoes the opening one level higher: the upward spiral.**
Zimmer: endings "can feel like an upward spiral." They circle back to the beginning, "yet reaching a higher place." Clark: "Let the ending echo the beginning." Steve Volk: "The best endings echo the beginning in some essential but surprising way." Hart's "kicker" should "sum up, surprise a little, perhaps bring things full circle." It should leave "no doubt that the story has ended." Clark's quick list: "Purposeful repetition links the parts." Schimel, as summarised in a lecture's slides: "the story comes back to its starting point, but that point has moved."
- *Example:* Brendan Borrell's ending (a researcher finds "new sp!!" in a predecessor's notes) was planned "from the first outline." His editor, Brendan Maher, then suggested a new opening scene that the ending could echo. Maher calls the ending's late new information "usually a no-no for story craft."
- *For a paper:* return to the cold-open clip or number with the viewer's new understanding ("now you can see why the gear walked out of mesh"). Repeat one key image or phrase at the start, the turn and the end.
- *Sources:* Zimmer, class notes; Krueger 2017; *Storycraft* via highlights; Meadows, ["Good Endings"](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2015/11/24/good-endings-how-to-write-a-kicker-your-editor-and-your-readers-will-love/), TON, 2015-11-24; Clark, Quick List. **Strength:** primary.

**P54. Keep the falling action short, and end with resolution plus forward motion.**
Hart: after the climax "the powerful engine of story has shut down." So "Wrap things up as quickly as possible and leave the stage." Clark: "Project the reader into the future." He also warns against endings that really say "I stopped writing here," and against stale ones "riding off into the sunset." Editor Becky Lang, via TON: "a sense of resolution and of propulsion."
- *For a paper:* after the verdict, limits and use get one tight chapter each, then a closing open question in the authors' framing. A long recap after the climax loses the viewer.
- *Sources:* *Storycraft* via highlights; Krueger 2017; Meadows 2015. **Strength:** primary.

**P55. Once the solution arrives, open no new problems.**
Olson: once you reach the solution, "your story ought to be on the home stretch." His example is Martin Luther King Jr.'s "I Have a Dream," whose second half is all THEREFORE.
- *For a paper:* after the verdict, the limits chapter states the known limits and opens no fresh mystery. New questions appear only at the very end, in the authors' future-work framing.
- *Source:* Olson, *The Narrative Gym*, chapter 5 (provenance flagged in P23). **Strength:** primary.

**P56. Where the caveats go: up front, or after the verdict?**
Schimel, through a reader's quote sheet: "Air your dirty laundry up front." Also: "resolutions are not the place for uncertainty." The existing doc's structure puts the limits in chapter 7, after the verdict.
- *For a paper:* scope limits that change how a number reads (simulation only, one course, preprint) go with the headline number. The late chapter keeps the remaining failures and the open questions.
- *Source:* Burgin 2016. **Strength:** secondary.

## G. The narrator as a guide

**P57. The intimate voice and the mobile stance.**
Kramer, rule 4: literary journalists write in "intimate voice," "informal, frank, human and ironic." Rule 6: they write from "a disengaged and mobile stance," and "tell stories and also turn and address readers directly." Readers experience the narrator as a "storytelling buddy." Rule 5: "Style counts, and tends to be plain and spare."
- *For a paper:* the narrator tells the story, steps out to explain ("here is why that matters"), and steps back in. It speaks as a knowledgeable colleague, not as a presenter or a hype voice.
- *Source:* Kramer 1995. **Strength:** primary.

**P58. A guide with a map, who advocates for the listener.**
Tim Requarth: a good guide is the "person that… has a roadmap for you" on "a confusing journey." Brendan Borrell, on Jon Mooallem: "he is going to be your personal tour guide." Andrew Grant: "you are the advocate for the reader, not for the scientists."
- *For a paper:* the narrator voices what an engineer would ask next and holds the paper to it ("but the paper doesn't test that"). It never advocates for the authors.
- *Sources:* Kim 2023; Rosen 2015; Holloway et al. 2017, TON. **Strength:** primary.

**P59. Write for a room of smart peers. Experts want it streamlined too.**
Mary Roach writes "to a large room full of people exactly like me." Wolchover: "There's no one alive who wants to be inundated with complex, challenging information." Scientists "one field away" still need writing aimed at a lay audience.
- *For a paper:* engineers outside the paper's subfield are novices on its specific parts (teaching doc L2). Streamline anyway.
- *Sources:* Missouri Review 2015 (Roach); Musser 2017 (Wolchover). **Strength:** primary.

**P60. A linear sequence that starts from the one fact the listener must have.**
Zinsser, in *On Writing Well*: imagine science writing as an upside-down pyramid. "Start at the bottom with the one fact a reader must know." Nowhere else, he says, must you "work so hard to write sentences that form a linear sequence."
- *For a paper:* each chapter begins from one fact without which nothing after it makes sense, and each sentence depends only on sentences already heard. Audio can't be re-read.
- *Source:* Popova, ["William Zinsser on How to Write Well About Science"](https://www.themarginalian.org/2015/05/27/william-zinsser-on-writing-well-science/), *The Marginalian*, 2015-05-27, quoting the book. **Strength:** primary words, quoted by a secondary source.

**P61. At points of complexity, go simpler. When the stakes are serious, understate.**
Clark's quick list: "Use shorter words, sentences and paragraphs at points of complexity." Another tool: "When the topic is most serious, understate."
- *For a paper:* the densest beat (the loss function, the update rule) gets the shortest sentences and a pause. The biggest result gets the plainest delivery, which matches the existing doc's finding N13 (no infotainment tone for experts).
- *Source:* Clark, Quick List, 2006. **Strength:** primary.

**P62. Meaning is built in the listener's sequence of realisations.**
Kramer, rule 8: literary journalists "develop meaning by building upon the readers' sequential reactions." "The readers' realizations are what the author and readers have made together." McPhee hopes readers "come to their own conclusions."
- *For a paper:* order the evidence so that the viewer reaches the insight a beat before the narrator states it (with a prediction prompt, teaching doc L16). Then confirm it with the source.
- *Sources:* Kramer 1995; McPhee, TON 2011. **Strength:** primary.

**P63. Classic style: the narrator shows an equal something real.**
Steven Pinker: "In classic style, prose is a window onto the world." The writer "has seen something in the world and he positions the reader" to see it. "The reader and writer are equals," and "the style is conversation." "Classic prose narrates ongoing events; we see agents performing actions that affect objects." His example is a generator warning. "Mild exposure to CO…" failed; "Using a generator indoors can kill you in minutes" works. Metadiscourse, as quoted, "is there to help the writer, not the reader."
- *For a paper:* point at the thing ("watch the left gear"), not at the discourse ("in this section we will discuss"). Give every sentence a doer, an action and an object. Treat the viewer as a peer who hasn't seen it yet.
- *Sources:* Pinker, talk transcript, Commonwealth Club, posted 2015-07-23 ([archive.org snapshot](https://web.archive.org/web/20150913/http://www.commonwealthclub.org/events/archive/transcript/steven-pinker) from 2015-09-13, read with Firecrawl); Shieber, ["Inaccessible writing in both senses of the term"](https://archive.blogs.harvard.edu/pamphlet/2014/09/29/inaccessible-writing-in-both-senses-of-the-term/), 2014-09-29 (quoting Pinker's *Chronicle* essay). **Strength:** primary (talk); secondary (Shieber).

**P64. The curse of knowledge, and the one reliable fix.**
Pinker: the writer "doesn't bother to explain the jargon or spell out the logic." That happens even "when writing for professional peers." His example: a study of the rabbit illusion described in jargon, against "a continuing series of taps running up the length of your arm." His reaction: "Well, why didn't they just say that?" The fix is to "show a draft… to a representative reader." Also: "You don't need abstract language simply because the concept is difficult."
- *For a paper:* every script gets a cold read by someone outside the subfield before recording. Each term they stumble on is defined at first use, or cut.
- *Sources:* Commonwealth Club transcript 2015; ["Exorcising the curse of knowledge"](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2012/11/exorcising-the-curse-of-knowledge), *Harvard Gazette*, 2012-11-08 (a report quoting him). **Strength:** primary (talk); secondary (report).

## H. Planning and editing tools

**P65. Write a theme statement first: subject, transitive verb, object.**
Hart's first line on any project is "THEME:" followed by a short subject-verb-object sentence, with a verb "preferably transitive because that shows causality." It "tells me what to leave in and what to leave out," and "what goes where." He credits Bill Blundell and Lajos Egri. Tom Hallman's story of Gary Wall had the theme "Action Creates Identity." Franklin's outlines, as described second-hand, use the same short active phrases: a complication, three development steps, a resolution. Changing the subject from "Woman" to "Ducker" changed the story.
- *For a paper:* write "THEME: [agent/team] [verb] [object]" before the outline ("Failure videos fix simulated designs"). Every chapter must serve it.
- *Sources:* Hart, ["Nut grafs: Getting to the heart of the story"](https://niemanstoryboard.org/2021/10/19/nut-grafs-getting-to-the-heart-of-the-story/), Nieman Storyboard, 2021-10-19; D'Agnese, ["The Franklin-Edgerton outlining method"](https://www.sleuthsayers.org/2024/07/the-franklin-edgerton-outlining-method.html), SleuthSayers, 2024-07-12. **Strength:** primary (Hart); secondary (Franklin).

**P66. Draw the structure, defend it, then hide it.**
McPhee's high-school teacher required "a structural presentation, an outline, or a doodle" with every piece. "Whatever it was, the structure had to be defended." McPhee moves index cards "to see where I'm going to find a good structure, a legitimate structure." He says structure "can have an attracting effect analogous to a story line in fiction." "Readers are not supposed to notice the structure." It should be "about as visible as someone's bones." Hart: once you think of a story as a series of scenic episodes, plotting the scenic structure becomes "the natural first step."
- *For a paper:* before scripting, draw the chapter diagram with each chapter's +/− and its closing tension. On air, don't announce it ("in part three we'll…").
- *Sources:* McPhee, TON 2011; Ulin, ["Draft No. 4: … master class in the writer's craft"](https://niemanstoryboard.org/stories/draft-no-4-the-legendary-john-mcphees-master-class-in-the-writers-craft/), Nieman Storyboard, 2017-12-21 (review quoting the book); Clark 2018; *Storycraft* via highlights. **Strength:** primary (McPhee's words, quoted).

**P67. Find the core in one sentence: the Dobzhansky template and the WSP model.**
Olson's template: "Nothing in _____ makes sense, except in the light of _____." The WSP model, as readers describe it, is "word, sentence, and paragraph":
- **Word:** the Dobzhansky template.
- **Sentence:** the ABT.
- **Paragraph:** the hero's journey, which UMCES summarises as "In an ordinary world… a flawed character… has a catalytic event."

Coats's #22 makes the same point: "What's the essence of your story? Most economical telling of it?"
- *For a paper:* before outlining, fill in "Nothing in [this result] makes sense except in the light of [this idea]." Then write the ABT sentence and the paragraph, and check all three against the theme statement (P65).
- *Sources:* *The Narrative Gym* (provenance flagged in P23); Dennison 2016 (UMCES); ["Randy Olson's Houston We Have a Narrative"](https://thetechnicalcommunicator.blogspot.com/2016/02/randy-olsons-houston-we-have-narrative_15.html), *The Technical Communicator* blog, 2016-02-15; Coats 2012. **Strength:** primary (the template); secondary (WSP; the *Houston* text was not read).

**P68. One point per paragraph: check the order with a logic map.**
Storr: "am I telling you one thing in this paragraph, and one thing alone?" As an editor, he has writers list one summary sentence per paragraph and reorder the piece to follow that list. Schimel, through the quote sheet: "Develop your story from the bottom up (data)," then "tell it from the top down."
- *For a paper:* write one sentence per narration paragraph and read the list on its own. If it doesn't tell the story in order, fix the order before polishing the prose.
- *Sources:* Sutton 2021; Burgin 2016. **Strength:** primary (Storr); secondary (Schimel).

## Where the guides agree

1. **Somebody does something, and it causes the next thing.** A story is a sequence of actions by someone who wants something (P1, P12, P49). Classic prose shows "agents performing actions that affect objects" (P63). Lists ("Here's a guy, and here's another guy"; AAA) are the named failure (P9, P23).
2. **One central question or problem.** This appears as Clark and French's engine (P42), Schimel's "one overarching question" (P22), Olson's single BUT and Dobzhansky template (P23, P67), Coats's #22, and Hart's three-word theme statement (P65).
3. **Explanation rides on an action line, in short doses.** Every science-story guide alternates scene and explanation and caps the time spent away from the action (P2, P4, P30, P44). Their names for it: Hart's layer cake, Kramer's "primary narrative with tales and digressions," French's "dessert and vegetables," Ananthaswamy's 3–4 paragraphs.
4. **Background only when the reader needs it, and only what serves the drama.** This is Hart's "enemy of narrative," Banaszynski's "right then," Wolchover's Jenga tower and Zinsser's "one fact" (P15, P46, P60).
5. **Move up and down the ladder.** Every abstraction gets a concrete instance, every instance points to a meaning, and the mechanism holds the middle (P27, P26, P32, P29).
6. **Never invent: no composites, no reordered chronology, no imputed thoughts, and say how you know** (P36, P37, P8, P39). Kramer, Hart, Harrington, Zimmer, Quammen, Rubiano and Kim all say this. Gutkind is the exception (conflict 2).
7. **Show character through action, not labels** (P38, P40, P35).
8. **The ending returns to the opening, changed.** Zimmer's upward spiral, Schimel's moved starting point, Clark's echo, Hart's kicker and Storr's *Jaws* ending all say it (P53).
9. **Know the ending and the structure before drafting, then hide the structure** (P52, P66, P65).
10. **Narrate as a plain-spoken peer.** Kramer's "intimate voice," Pinker's "conversation" between equals, Roach's room of people like her, and Clark's "understate" all describe it (P57, P63, P59, P61).

## Where they conflict

1. **Withhold, or say where it's going.**
   - *Withhold:* Wolchover is "always holding something back" and ends sections so the reader "can't bail." Storr waits until "the third quarter" for complexity. Clark says "make them wait."
   - *Say where it's going:* Zimmer and Updike say telling readers where the story is going "primes them." Schimel's faster structures put the answer up front, and Pinker's classic writer simply shows what he has seen.
   - *Resolution for us:* withhold the *how* and the *cost*, not the *what*. State the question and the headline outcome early (P45). Write explanation paragraphs point-first (P22). Keep suspense for the turns (P20, P44). This also fits the existing doc's expertise-reversal caution.
2. **Imagining thoughts.**
   - Gutkind's 2023 essay says you "can… imagine… what someone might have been thinking" as long as you don't "violate the reader's trust."
   - Kramer allows no thoughts "unless the sources have said they'd had those very thoughts." Harrington and Hart agree.
   - *Our rule follows Kramer* (P36). The existing doc's "never allowed" list already does.
3. **The middle of the ladder.**
   - Clark: "Beware of the middle."
   - TON's science version: the middle rungs are "where writers often have to do the most explanatory writing," so they must be "sturdy."
   - *For a paper,* the mechanism lives in the middle. The rule is to anchor each middle-rung explanation to a bottom-rung instance, not to avoid the middle (P27).
4. **How much scene, and how long away from it.**
   - Gutkind expects most of the text to be scene, and Hart wants about 500 words for a full scene.
   - Nijhuis says two sentences "can do a lot."
   - Blundell allows two paragraphs of digression and Ananthaswamy 3–4. Sandra Blakeslee writes "eight or nine paragraphs" of hard science before "something more entertaining," which Tom French calls "dessert and vegetables".
   - There is no consensus number (P30). Ours has to come from retention data.
5. **Cutting scenes that aren't needed.**
   - Zimmer: an unnecessary scene "has to go."
   - Wolchover keeps details "just because they're compelling or beautiful."
   - McPhee's "Writing is selection" sides with Zimmer (P31).
6. **Outline first, or let the structure emerge.**
   - *Outline first:* McPhee, Hart, Franklin and Yong.
   - *Let it emerge:* Aschwanden ("I can't outline it in advance") and Quammen ("I don't make an outline"). Both are TON interviews read by a sub-agent.
   - For a scripted video that must be reviewed, outline first (P66).
7. **Chronology or theme.**
   - Tom French and Hart default to chronology and are wary of flashbacks.
   - McPhee prefers theme when chronology "would be misleading."
   - Zimmer allows any jump if readers "see why you make every jump" (P19).
8. **A clean resolution, or honest puzzlement.**
   - Hart's arc ends in a point of insight and a resolution, and Wolchover wants "a really satisfying resolution to the tension."
   - David Dobbs, interviewing Quammen, prefers real "mystification" to "forced epiphanies." Leonhardt ends on "multiple explanations."
   - *For a paper:* resolve the engine question only as far as the paper does, and say what remains open (P17, P47, P10).
9. **Questions.**
   - Zimmer: "Avoid rhetorical questions."
   - Clark, French, Strauss, Glass and Mukherjee build their structures on questions.
   - These are compatible. The question is the *structure* (P42). A rhetorical question as filler ("But what does this mean?") is the failure.
10. **Where the caveats go.**
    - Schimel: "Air your dirty laundry up front," and keep uncertainty out of the resolution.
    - Hart wants a short falling action. The existing doc puts the limits after the verdict.
    - *Resolution:* scope limits go with the headline number, and remaining failures go after it (P56, P54).
11. **One problem, or many tries.**
    - Olson: a second "but" starts "a second narrative."
    - Hart's rising action is a rise and fall of hope, Nijhuis names "Repeated Attempts," and the existing doc uses Sanderson's try-fail cycles.
    - *Resolution:* one central problem, with each try nested under it as a step against that same problem (P23, P18, P16).
12. **Does a story need a protagonist who changes?**
    - Franklin, Hart's "story narrative" and Storr centre a character's change.
    - Hart says "Explanatory narratives often lack something as basic as a protagonist." Carpenter says science stories "seldom have a complete narrative thru-line." Storr: transformation "is not a prerequisite."
    - *For a paper:* the documented belief is what changes, not a person (P13, P5, P8).
13. **Signposting.**
    - Pinker: metadiscourse helps "the writer, not the reader."
    - McPhee: structure should be "about as visible as someone's bones."
    - Clark's 2002 tools allow sub-headlines to "index the story for the readers," and a sub-agent found Stuart Shieber arguing that advance signposting helps in long works (opinion).
    - This bears on the existing doc's 10-second roadmap spoken as questions. Keep it short, and phrase it as questions, not as an agenda.
14. **Pixar's "Combine characters" (#5)** is a composite, which Kramer forbids. Take only "Simplify. Focus… Hop over detours" from it (P24).
15. **Does narrative even help insiders?** Olson says an "inner circle" is "fine with" AAA, and that "The ABT is only about form," not truth. This matches the existing doc's finding that expertise shrinks the benefit (N4, N5). Story structure carries attention and order. It guarantees neither understanding nor accuracy (P23, P25).

## A storyteller's checklist for a research-paper story

An editor should be able to answer each question with yes or no from the script and its source map. Technique numbers are in brackets.

1. **Whose story, and what stops them?** In one line: who acts (the team), what they want (the paper's stated goal), and the first *documented* thing that stopped them. Is the robot or the model kept as an instrument, never as the one who wants? [P1, P39, P8, P9]
2. **Theme and engine.** Is there a written THEME line (subject, transitive verb, object)? Is there one engine question stated in the first 30 seconds and answered at the verdict? Does every chapter visibly serve both? [P65, P42, P67, P23]
3. **One tracked thing.** Does the script follow one named robot, design, run or failure case through the method and the results? Does it return to that thing at the open, at the turn and at the end? [P3, P18, P53]
4. **Action line vs digression.** Tag every paragraph **A** (someone did something, with a result) or **D** (explanation). Is there ever a run of more than 3–4 D paragraphs? Does every D answer a question the A just before it raised? [P2, P4, P30, P46]
5. **Digression timing.** Does each mechanism explanation start while an outcome is pending (the clip paused before the result), not after the outcome has been shown? [P44, P20]
6. **Causal joints.** Does each chapter open on a decision or event caused by the previous result, with the "because" sourced? Can any chapter open with "Now let's look at…"? If so, rewrite it. [P12, P16, P15]
7. **The hope chart.** Mark each chapter + (it helped) or − (it exposed a problem). Is there a − after the first +? Is there any chapter where nothing changes position (merge it or cut it)? Is every way out causal, with no lucky breaks? [P16, P17, P24]
8. **The ladder.** For each chapter, can you point to a bottom-rung item (a clip, a part, a number with its denominator), the top-rung sentence (the one insight) and the mechanism between them? Underline paragraphs with no concrete noun: those are stuck in the middle. [P27, P26, P32]
9. **Pace where it matters.** Time the script. Do the decisive moments (the first failure, the reversal, the verdict) get the most screen time and the slowest delivery? Does setup and boilerplate get the least? Is each problem shown as one singular moment? [P28, P29, P34]
10. **No invention.** Can every sentence about what the team thought, expected or felt be traced to their own published words, with a spoken attribution? Are there zero verbs of desire or feeling for robots or models, no composite or reordered events, and no "surprising" without a source? [P36, P37, P33, P41]
11. **Show, don't label.** Is every adjective about people ("meticulous," "brilliant") replaced by the action or number that shows it? Do the authors' own quotes do work (a decision, a doubt, a surprise) rather than praise? [P38, P40]
12. **Setups and payoffs.** List every term, clip and number introduced in the first third. Is each one used later? Is the cold open's promise answered no later than the chapter that promised it? Does anything framed as minor turn out major? Do the scope limits that change the headline's meaning appear with the headline? [P51, P45, P43, P56]
13. **Gold coins in the middle.** Does the middle third hold at least two sourced high points (a striking clip, a counter-intuitive ablation, a candid author line) that were not spent in the cold open? [P48, P47]
14. **The ending.** Was the last minute written first? Does it return to the opening image or number at a higher level? Does it open no new problem, stay within about two minutes of the verdict, and end on the authors' open question? [P52, P53, P54, P55]
15. **The narrator and the curse of knowledge.**
    - Has someone outside the paper's subfield cold-read the script, and has every term they tripped on been fixed?
    - Is there any sentence about the discourse ("in this section") instead of about the thing?
    - Are the shortest sentences at the hardest beat?
    - Does a one-sentence-per-paragraph list read as the story in order?

    [P63, P64, P61, P60, P57, P68]
